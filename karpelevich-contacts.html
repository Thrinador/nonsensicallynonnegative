One contact blocks a fixed expansion.
Extremality forces every image to touch.

An invariant polygon can have plenty of room inside it. Its rotated, contracted image can sit comfortably away from every edge.

An extremal eigenvalue behaves differently. At its least realizing order, every image vertex must touch the polygon’s boundary. A theorem of Dmitriev and Dynkin then organizes those contacts: every vertex looks toward the same pair of cyclic neighbors.

This is the missing step between the matrix–polygon correspondence and Karpelevich’s chip game. We will prove the no-interior-contact conclusion, then establish the global contact ordering and its consistent endpoint convention before working through an exact extremal example.

Two hypotheses do different jobs

Write $\mathcal K_n$ for the eigenvalues of stochastic matrices of order $n$. Recall the geometric translation:

$$\lambda\in\mathcal K_n\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad \lambda Q\subseteq Q\text{ for a nontrivial convex hull with at most }n\text{ vertices}.$$

Throughout the main argument, $\lambda$ is nonreal. Its invariant polygons have the origin in their interior.

We need two additional hypotheses:

Hypothesis Exact meaning Its role
Radial extremality at order $n$ $\lambda\in\mathcal K_n$ and $c\lambda\notin\mathcal K_n$ for every real $c>1$ Rules out an outward improvement along the same ray
New at order $n$ $\lambda\notin\mathcal K_{n-1}$ Rules out a realization with fewer states or fewer polygon vertices

In particular, every witness with at most $n$ vertices must have exactly $n$ vertices. Otherwise the reverse construction from Stop 1 would put $\lambda$ in $\mathcal K_{n-1}$.

The order matters. Padding a smaller matrix with an identity block does not produce a new least-order configuration. Nor can we apply the contact theorem merely because a point lies somewhere on the topological boundary: the hypothesis here is specifically radial extremality.

If the whole image is interior, expand it

Suppose every image vertex lies strictly inside $Q$. There are finitely many vertices and edges, so a small outward expansion still fits:

$$\lambda Q\subset\operatorname{int}Q \quad\Longrightarrow\quad (1+\varepsilon)\lambda Q\subseteq Q \quad\text{for some }\varepsilon>0.$$

The same polygon now realizes a larger eigenvalue on the same ray. Such a $\lambda$ cannot be radially extremal.

A finite-inequality proof of the expansion claim

Write the polygon as an intersection of supporting half-planes,

$$Q=\{z:h_\ell(z)\leq b_\ell\text{ for every edge }\ell\},$$

where each $h_\ell$ is a real linear functional. The origin is interior, so each $b_\ell>0$. If the vertices are $z_i$, strict containment says

$$h_\ell(\lambda z_i)<b_\ell\qquad\text{for every }i,\ell.$$

These are finitely many strict inequalities. By continuity they remain valid when each left side is multiplied by $1+\varepsilon$ for sufficiently small $\varepsilon>0$. Taking convex hulls proves the claim.

This argument only shows that at least one contact is necessary. If one image point already touches an edge, expanding the whole image may fail immediately, even while other image vertices are interior. Getting every image vertex onto the boundary requires an additional argument.

Why even one interior image is impossible

Here is the stronger conclusion, under both hypotheses from Section 1:

$$\boxed{\lambda z_i\in\partial Q\qquad\text{for every vertex }z_i.}$$

One way to understand it is to allow the polygon to move while the multiplier moves. If an image vertex is interior, its convex-combination equation has room to adjust. We can keep the other matrix rows fixed, solve their equations continuously, and use that room to close the remaining row for a slightly larger multiplier.

The following proof makes that idea exact. It is an analytic explanation of the no-interior-contact conclusion; the stronger cyclic organization comes afterward.

A complete proof using one adjustable matrix row

Suppose $\lambda z_i$ is interior. Choose stochastic realizing rows for the polygon and call the matrix $P$. This matrix must be irreducible. Otherwise a smaller diagonal block containing $\lambda$ has spectral radius $\rho\leq1$. Stochastic normalization, as in Stop 1, realizes $\lambda/\rho$ at that smaller order. If $\rho=1$, this contradicts least order; if $\rho<1$, it contradicts radial extremality. Here $\rho>0$ because $\lambda$ is nonreal.

Delete row and column $i$, leaving a nonnegative principal submatrix $B$. Then $\rho(B)<1$. To see this directly, regard $P$ as a finite irreducible Markov chain. From every remaining state there is a positive-probability path to the deleted state. For some common finite number of steps, every row of the corresponding power of $B$ has sum strictly less than one. Hence $\rho(B)<1$.

Also $\lambda\notin\operatorname{spec}B$. Otherwise $\rho(B)>0$ and the nonnegative matrix $B/\rho(B)$, of order $n-1$, would realize $\lambda/\rho(B)$ after stochastic normalization. Since $\rho(B)<1$, that is an outward improvement along the same ray, contradicting extremality at order $n$.

No polygon vertex is zero, so scale all coordinates to make $z_i=1$. Let $z_J$ collect the other coordinates, and let $c$ be column $i$ of $P$ with its $i$th entry removed. The unchanged rows say

$$\lambda z_J=Bz_J+c.$$

For a complex number $\mu$ sufficiently close to $\lambda$, define

$$v_i(\mu)=1,\qquad v_J(\mu)=(\mu I-B)^{-1}c.$$

These coordinates vary continuously and equal the original coordinates at $\mu=\lambda$. All rows except row $i$ already satisfy the eigenvector equation for $\mu$.

Originally $\lambda v_i(\lambda)=\lambda$ is strictly inside the hull of the coordinates. Interior membership persists when both the point and these finitely many vertices move sufficiently little. Therefore, for all sufficiently close $\mu$,

$$\mu\in\operatorname{conv}\{v_1(\mu),\ldots,v_n(\mu)\}.$$

Choose convex coefficients for this last inclusion as row $i$. Together with the unchanged rows they form a stochastic matrix having eigenvalue $\mu$. In particular, choose $\mu=(1+\varepsilon)\lambda$ for small $\varepsilon>0$. This contradicts radial extremality and proves the claim.

For completeness, the stability assertion follows from supporting functionals: a small disk centered at $\lambda$ lies in the original hull. Moving every vertex by less than half the disk’s radius changes every unit supporting value by at most that amount, leaving a smaller disk available for the moving point.

This explains why the least-order hypothesis is useful even in a geometric statement: it prevents the realizing matrix from splitting into smaller pieces when we try to move it.

A supporting edge forces a sparse row

Now let an image point $\lambda z_i$ lie on an edge $[z_a,z_b]$. Choose a supporting functional $h$ with

$$h(z)\leq b\text{ on }Q,\qquad h(\lambda z_i)=b.$$

In any stochastic representation of this image,

$$\lambda z_i=\sum_jp_{ij}z_j,$$

we obtain

$$0=b-h(\lambda z_i)=\sum_jp_{ij}\bigl(b-h(z_j)\bigr).$$

Every summand is nonnegative. A vertex off that supporting edge has $b-h(z_j)>0$, so its coefficient must vanish. Only the two endpoints can participate:

$$\lambda z_i=\alpha_i z_a+\beta_i z_b,\qquad \alpha_i,\beta_i\geq0,\qquad\alpha_i+\beta_i=1.$$

If the image is itself a vertex, its row has just one nonzero entry. For a fixed eigenvector polygon, the coefficients are now determined by the image point’s position on its supporting face.

Figure 2. In the exact example below, $\lambda z_1$ is the midpoint of $[z_2,z_0]$. A positive coefficient at $z_1$, which lies strictly off that supporting line, would pull the average away from the line. Its coefficient must be zero.

We have obtained a row with at most two nonzero entries. We still have to determine which edge each image can occupy.

The fixed cyclic shift: a complete contact argument

Two elementary toolsEvery closed edge has a contactOne half-open convention works around the entire polygonCounting contacts gives the fixed shift

The supporting-line calculation identifies the two possible targets in each row. We now prove that the target edges advance one step whenever the source vertex advances one step. This is the global part of the Dmitriev–Dynkin contact theorem.

Keep the hypotheses already stated: $\lambda$ is a nonreal radial extremum in $\mathcal K_n\setminus\mathcal K_{n-1}$, and $Q$ is an invariant polygon. It has exactly $n$ genuine vertices. The no-interior-image lemma applies to every invariant polygon with at most $n$ vertices, including the polygons obtained by the small changes below.

First suppose $|\lambda|<1$, and put $P=\lambda Q$. Both polygons have $n$ vertices, and every vertex of $P$ lies on $\partial Q$. All contour arcs and edge endpoints below are ordered counterclockwise. A boundary piece of $Q$ is free if it does not meet $P$.

Two elementary tools

Contact-count lemma. Let $a,b\in\partial Q\cap\partial P$. Suppose each endpoint is either a vertex of both polygons or an interior point of an edge of both polygons. The counterclockwise arcs from $a$ to $b$ contain the same number of vertices of the two polygons, with the endpoints counted consistently.

To prove this, let the two counts be $N_Q,N_P$. Replace the outer arc by the inner one. The resulting convex polygon $Q_-$ satisfies

$$P\subseteq Q_-\subseteq Q,\qquad \lambda Q_-\subseteq P\subseteq Q_-.$$

Its vertex count is $n-N_Q+N_P$. Minimality of the realizing order therefore gives $N_Q\leq N_P$. Conversely, replace the corresponding arc of $P$ by the outer arc, obtaining $P_+$ with

$$P\subseteq P_+\subseteq Q.$$

The polygon $Q_+=\lambda^{-1}P_+$ contains $Q$, and

$$\lambda Q_+=P_+\subseteq Q\subseteq Q_+.$$

Its vertex count is $n-N_P+N_Q$, giving the reverse inequality. The endpoint assumption makes the two splice counts exact: no unmatched corner is introduced at a splice. Convexity follows from nesting and the common supporting edges or common corner cones at the endpoints.

Corner-cut lemma. Let $x,y,z$ be consecutive vertices of $Q$. If the half-open edge $[y,z)$ is free, then

$$\lambda[x,y]\subseteq\partial Q.$$

Indeed, move $y$ a sufficiently small distance toward $x$ on $[x,y]$, leaving the other vertices fixed. Call the new vertex $y_\varepsilon$ and the new polygon $Q_\varepsilon$. The removed triangular sliver meets $P$ at most at $z$, so for a small enough move

$$P\subseteq Q_\varepsilon\subseteq Q,\qquad \lambda Q_\varepsilon\subseteq P\subseteq Q_\varepsilon.$$

The assertion about the sliver follows by checking the finitely many edges of $P$: those not through $z$ have positive clearance from a sufficiently thin sliver, and an edge through $z$ has a strict angle from the free half-edge.

Now $\lambda y_\varepsilon$ lies strictly between $\lambda x$ and $\lambda y$. If that segment were not on one supporting edge of $Q$, its open segment would be interior to $Q$. The sliver has not reached it, so $\lambda y_\varepsilon$ would be interior to $Q_\varepsilon$, contradicting the no-interior-image lemma. This proves the assertion. Reflecting the argument gives its companion: if $(y,z]$ is free, the image of the edge following $[y,z]$ lies on $\partial Q$.

Proof figure. Closed edges and the two half-open conventions used in the counting argument. These are definitions of the gap types, rather than a claimed mixed-contact eigenpolygon.

Every closed edge has a contact

Let $B(Q)$ be the number of free closed edges. It is an integer between zero and $n$. Among all invariant $n$-gons choose one maximizing $B(Q)$. We will prove its maximum is zero, which proves the claim for every invariant polygon.

Suppose $[y_0,z_0]$ is free, and write $x_0,y_0,z_0,u_0$ for the adjacent four vertices. By the corner-cut lemma, the segments $\lambda[x_0,y_0]$ and $\lambda[z_0,u_0]$ lie in edges of $Q$. Call these edges $[x_1,y_1]$ and $[z_1,u_1]$.

Choose a point in the interior of each of those image segments. Each chosen point is interior to an edge of both polygons. The inner arc between them has just the two corners $\lambda y_0,\lambda z_0$. By the contact-count lemma, the outer arc also has just two corners. Thus $y_1,z_1$ are consecutive: the two containing edges are separated by the single edge $[y_1,z_1]$.

We claim this separating edge is free. Cut a thin strip from $Q$ parallel to the original free edge $[y_0,z_0]$. Because that closed edge is disjoint from compact $P$, the strip can be chosen with $P$ still contained in the resulting polygon. Its two new corners lie on $[x_0,y_0]$ and $[z_0,u_0]$.

In the image polygon the corresponding corners move away from the separating edge along $[x_1,y_1]$ and $[z_1,u_1]$. They were its only possible image contacts: the count above leaves no intervening image vertex. Thus $[y_1,z_1]$ becomes free. Existing free edges remain free for a sufficiently small cut; the shortened edges stay on their old lines, the replacement parallel edge has positive clearance, and the image polygon has shrunk. If $[y_1,z_1]$ had not already been free, this would increase $B(Q)$, contrary to maximality.

Repeat the construction from the new free edge, in the same fixed polygon. At each step,

$$\lambda[x_j,y_j]\subseteq[x_{j+1},y_{j+1}].$$

There are only finitely many edges, so some edge repeats. For some positive integer $h$ this gives $\lambda^h E\subseteq E$ for an edge $E$ of $Q$. Iterating would put $\lambda^{mh}E$ in $E$ for every $m$. Since $|\lambda|<1$, these segments converge to $0$, so the closed edge would contain $0$. That contradicts $0\in\operatorname{int}Q$.

Consequently $B(Q)=0$ for every invariant polygon: every closed edge meets $P$.

One half-open convention works around the entire polygon

Writing $v_i = z_i$ for the indexed vertices of $Q$ (so $x_0, y_0, z_0, u_0, v_0, w_0$ can denote consecutive corners along a local arc), let $B_+(Q)$ count free edges of the form $[v_i,v_{i+1})$, and let $B_-(Q)$ count free edges of the form $(v_i,v_{i+1}]$. We prove

$$\boxed{B_+(Q)=0\quad\text{or}\quad B_-(Q)=0.}$$

Again use a finite integer maximum, now maximizing $B_+(Q)B_-(Q)$ over the invariant polygons. Suppose the maximum is positive. Choose a free pair

$$[y_0,z_0),\qquad (u_0,v_0]$$

for which the counterclockwise arc from $z_0$ to $u_0$ has the fewest edges; call this number $h$. The case $z_0=u_0$ means $h=0$.

Let $x_0$ precede $y_0$ and $w_0$ follow $v_0$. The corner-cut lemma puts $\lambda[x_0,y_0]$ in an edge $[x_1,y_1]$ and $\lambda[v_0,w_0]$ in an edge $[v_1,w_1]$. Let $z_1$ follow $y_1$, and let $u_1$ precede $v_1$.

The fact that every closed edge has a contact gives

$$\lambda z_0\in(y_1,z_1],\qquad \lambda u_0\in[u_1,v_1).$$

For example, if $\lambda y_0$ is interior to $[x_1,y_1]$, the next closed edge could not have a contact if the next image vertex $\lambda z_0$ lay beyond $z_1$. If $\lambda y_0=y_1$, first make the small corner cut at $y_0$ and pass to the limit. The right-hand assertion follows in reverse order.

Apply the contact-count lemma between interior points of the two coincident end-edge segments. It shows that the arc $[z_1,u_1]$ also has $h$ edges.

This arc contains a vertex shared by $P$ and $Q$. Otherwise, for $h>0$, each of its $h$ closed edges would need an image vertex in its interior. But the inner arc $[\lambda z_0,\lambda u_0]$ has only $h+1$ vertices, and both endpoints lie outside the open arc $(z_1,u_1)$. Only $h-1$ vertices are available there, a contradiction. For $h=0$, the two displayed endpoint containments already force the common point.

Let $f$ and $g$ be the first and last shared vertices in $[z_1,u_1]$. If $e$ precedes $f$, then $[e,f)$ is free. Here is the count when $[z_1,f]$ has $q>0$ edges: the contact-count lemma gives $q+1$ image vertices on $[\lambda z_0,f]$. One is $\lambda z_0$, before $z_1$, and one is $f$. Every one of the first $q-1$ edges needs an interior contact because its endpoints are not shared. These use all the remaining $q-1$ image vertices. None is left for $[e,f)$.

If $q=0$, then $f=z_1$ and the same endpoint count gives $\lambda z_0=z_1$. The only way $[y_1,z_1)$ could still meet $P$ is the edge coincidence starting at $\lambda y_0=y_1$. A small corner cut at $y_0$ breaks that coincidence and frees $[y_1,z_1)$ while preserving all previously free half-edges. It would increase the positive product $B_+B_-$, which is impossible. Thus the assertion also holds when $q=0$. Applying the reverse argument at the other end gives a free edge $(g,j]$, where $j$ follows $g$.

Our minimal choice of $h$ now says that $[f,g]$ has at least $h$ edges. It is contained in the $h$-edge arc $[z_1,u_1]$. Hence

$$f=z_1,\qquad g=u_1.$$

The endpoint counts also force

$$\lambda z_0=z_1,\qquad\lambda u_0=u_1.$$

Finally, $[y_1,z_1)$ and $(u_1,v_1]$ themselves must be free. If the first were not, moving $y_0$ slightly toward $x_0$ would free it: the only possible remaining contact is the edge coincidence starting at $\lambda y_0=y_1$. The cut preserves all previously free half-edges and makes this additional one free. Since both counts are positive, their product increases, contradicting maximality. The reverse cut at $v_0$ proves the other assertion.

We have therefore produced another free pair at the same minimal separation, with $z_1=\lambda z_0$. Repeating inside the same polygon gives vertices

$$z_0,\lambda z_0,\lambda^2z_0,\ldots$$

of $Q$. Some vertex must repeat, which would give $\lambda^m z_0=z_0$ for a positive $m$. This is impossible because $z_0\ne0$ and $|\lambda|<1$. The maximum product is zero, proving the boxed alternative for every invariant polygon.

Counting contacts gives the fixed shift

Suppose first that no edge of the form $(v_j,v_{j+1}]$ is free. These $n$ half-open edges partition the boundary, and each contains an image vertex. There are exactly $n$ image vertices. Consequently each contains exactly one.

Multiplication by $\lambda$ preserves cyclic order. The unique contact assigned to the next half-open edge must therefore come from the next source vertex. For a single fixed integer $k$,

$$\lambda v_i\in(v_{i+k-1},v_{i+k}],$$

or equivalently

$$\boxed{\lambda v_i=\alpha_i v_{i+k-1}+\beta_i v_{i+k}, \qquad\alpha_i\geq0,\quad\beta_i>0,\quad\alpha_i+\beta_i=1.}$$

If the other half-open convention is the one with no free edges, we obtain the same formula with every $\alpha_i>0$ and $\beta_i\geq0$. In particular, a configuration cannot contain both a zero alpha coefficient and a zero beta coefficient. Reflection across the real axis exchanges the conventions, giving the orientation used in the chip game.

The value of the shift also agrees with the angle. Let $\gamma_i$ be the positive exterior turn from edge $[v_i,v_{i+1}]$ to the next edge. The direction of its image edge lies between the directions of the two target edges containing its endpoints. On consistent angular lifts this gives

$$\gamma_i+\cdots+\gamma_{i+k-2} \leq\arg\lambda \leq\gamma_i+\cdots+\gamma_{i+k-1}.$$

Sum over $i$. Each exterior turn occurs $k-1$ times on the left and $k$ times on the right, and the total exterior turn is $2\pi$. Thus

$$\frac{2\pi(k-1)}n\leq\arg\lambda\leq\frac{2\pi k}n.$$

In the strict-contraction extremal case the inequalities are strict. Equality would put $\lambda$ on the ray of an $n$th root of unity, which is already realizable with modulus one. The smaller modulus could not be radially extremal.

If $|\lambda|=1$, area and containment give $\lambda Q=Q$. Multiplication permutes the vertices. Its orbit gives a regular polygon with as many vertices as the order of the root of unity; least-order minimality makes that order $n$ and makes $Q$ that regular $n$-gon. The fixed-shift conclusion follows directly. This handles the unit-circle case separately from the contraction arguments above.

We have now proved the global contact pattern, including the consistent endpoint convention. The two nonzero cyclic diagonals are a consequence of minimal order and extremality, rather than an extra assumption imposed on the matrix.

An exact extremal polygon

Set

$$\lambda=-\frac{1+i}{2},\qquad z_0=1,\quad z_1=\frac i2,\quad z_2=-\frac{1+i}{2}.$$

The three vertices are numbered counterclockwise. Direct multiplication gives

$$\lambda z_0=z_2,\qquad \lambda z_1=\frac12z_2+\frac12z_0,\qquad \lambda z_2=z_1.$$

So this is an invariant triangle, with realizing matrix

$$P=\begin{pmatrix}0&0&1\\1/2&0&1/2\\0&1&0\end{pmatrix}.$$

It is genuinely extremal at order three. A real stochastic $3\times3$ matrix with nonreal eigenvalue $\mu$ has spectrum $1,\mu,\overline\mu$. Its nonnegative diagonal gives

$$0\leq\operatorname{tr}P=1+2\operatorname{Re}\mu.$$

Our $\lambda$ has real part $-1/2$. Every outward multiple $c\lambda$, $c>1$, violates that trace inequality. And $\lambda$ is new at order three: the second eigenvalue of a stochastic $2\times2$ matrix is real.

Figure 3. Here $n=3$ and $k=2$. The three target edges are $[z_1,z_2]$, $[z_2,z_0]$, and $[z_0,z_1]$. Two images hit the second endpoint; the remaining image is a midpoint. The modulus is $1/\sqrt2$.

The contact coefficients are

$$\alpha=(0,1/2,0),\qquad\beta=(1,1/2,1).$$

Every $\beta_i$ is positive, exactly as the game convention requires.

Every displayed multiplier is radially extremal at order three: it has real part −1/2, while every outward multiple violates the trace inequality. The family stops before t = 3/4, where nonreality and the triangle would be lost. Rounded displays illustrate the exact formulas proved below.

The exact boundary family behind the explorer

For $0\leq t<3/4$, take the lower nonreal root

$$\lambda_t=\frac{-1-i\sqrt{3-4t}}2,\qquad \lambda_t^2+\lambda_t+1-t=0.$$

Use the counterclockwise vertices $(1,\lambda_t^2,\lambda_t)$. Their realizing matrix and coefficients are

$$P_t=\begin{pmatrix}0&0&1\\1-t&0&t\\0&1&0\end{pmatrix},\qquad \alpha=(0,t,0),\qquad\beta=(1,1-t,1).$$

The identity $\lambda_t^3=(1-t)+t\lambda_t$ closes the middle row. The same trace argument proves radial extremality for every member of this nonreal family. At $t=0$ the multiplier is a cube root of unity and all contacts are vertex contacts. For $0<t<3/4$ it is a strict contraction with one edge contact. The endpoint $t=3/4$ is excluded: the multiplier becomes real and the triangle degenerates.

Why the choice of orientation matters

Reflect the worked example across the real axis. Its eigenvalue is now $(-1+i)/2$. Counterclockwise order becomes

$$w_0=1,\qquad w_1=\overline\lambda,\qquad w_2=\overline\lambda^{\,2}=-\frac i2.$$

Again $k=2$, but this time

$$\alpha=(1,1,1/2),\qquad\beta=(0,0,1/2).$$

The first two contacts are now at the first endpoint of their designated edges. We cannot simply declare those zero $\beta$ coefficients positive while retaining the same counterclockwise convention.

The theorem’s no-mixed-zero conclusion provides the remedy: choose the conjugate orientation in which every $\beta_i>0$. The explorer lets you see the endpoint roles switch. No eigenvalue is lost from the eventual description, since the region is symmetric under conjugation.

More generally, reflection followed by counterclockwise renumbering swaps the two coefficient families and changes the shift to $n-k+1$. The game can use $1\leq k<n$: if the provisional shift is $n$, nonreality forces all first-endpoint coefficients to be positive, and reflection supplies shift $1$ with every second-endpoint coefficient positive.

The board is finally defined

In this chosen orientation, the contact equation reads

$$\lambda z_i=\alpha_i z_{i+k-1}+\beta_i z_{i+k},\qquad\beta_i>0.$$

The contact theorem applies to every invariant witness with at most $n$ vertices for this same extremal $\lambda$. This makes it reusable during the proof. Whenever a replacement satisfies

$$\lambda Q\subseteq Q'\subseteq Q,$$

we automatically have $\lambda Q'\subseteq Q'$. If $Q'$ has at most $n$ vertices, least order forces it to have exactly $n$, and the contact conclusions apply again. A valid invariant variation cannot leave an image vertex strictly interior.

Karpelevich’s game now has its geometric foundation: a legal move replaces a corner while preserving the multiplier, and the resulting contacts still obey the theorem. The next stop uses those moves to collect scattered edge contacts into a simpler configuration.
