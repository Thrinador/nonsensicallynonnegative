So far, our polygons have been witnesses. To prove that a particular number $\lambda$ is a stochastic eigenvalue, we find a polygon $Q$ with at most $n$ vertices such that $\lambda Q\subseteq Q$.

Karpelevich now turns that picture into a description of the region itself. He finds special polygons $C$ for which every point of $C$ is a stochastic eigenvalue, using $C$ as its own witness. His Theorem A says that these polygons, taken together, cover the whole Karpelevich region.

The extra property is simple to state:

$$z,w\in C\quad\Longrightarrow\quad zw\in C.$$

The interesting question is how the contact relations from the earlier posts produce polygons with this property.

What “cyclic” means here

Choose a complex number $\mu$ and a positive integer $h$, and write $\varepsilon=e^{2\pi i/h}$. Consider all the points

$$\mu^m\varepsilon^j,\qquad m=0,1,2,\ldots,\qquad j=0,\ldots,h-1.$$

Start with the powers of $\mu$, then include their $h$ rotated copies. When their closed convex hull is a finite polygon, call it $C(\mu,h)$. This is the power-generated, rotational meaning of cyclic polygon used in this post.

We count actual vertices of the hull. A list of eight generators might have fewer than eight vertices. Also, taking the first few powers does not automatically give the hull of the entire infinite orbit: the next power might fall outside.

The closed-hull convention includes real-axis segments. In the nondegenerate examples below it agrees with the ordinary convex hull of the infinite generating set. Throughout the union statement we take $n\geq2$.

One closing relation captures an infinite orbit

Here is a finite certificate. Form

$$P=\operatorname{conv}\{\mu^m\varepsilon^j: 0\leq m<L,\ 0\leq j<h\}.$$

Suppose the next power satisfies

$$\boxed{\mu^L\in P.}$$

Then $P=C(\mu,h)$, and it has at most $hL$ vertices.

To see this, multiplication by $\varepsilon$ permutes the generating list, so $\varepsilon P=P$. Multiplication by $\mu$ advances every generator to the next power. The only images needing a check are the terminal ones, $\varepsilon^j\mu^L$; they lie in $P$ by the boxed condition and rotational symmetry. Hence

$$\mu P\subseteq P.$$

Induction now puts every later power and every rotated copy inside $P$. The finite hull already contains the entire infinite orbit.

Our four-state example from the roadmap makes this concrete. Put

$$\mu=\frac{-1+i\sqrt{11}}4.$$

Direct calculation gives

$$\mu^4=\frac38+\frac58\mu.$$

Thus the next power lies on the segment joining $1$ to $\mu$. With $h=1$ and $L=4$,

$$C(\mu,1)=\operatorname{conv}\{1,\mu,\mu^2,\mu^3\}.$$

Figure 1. The amber point $\mu^4$ lies on $[1,\mu]$. Multiplication advances the first three generators and sends the fourth onto that edge. Every later power stays in the same quadrilateral.

Why every point in a cyclic polygon is realizable

Products of orbit points are orbit points:

$$\bigl(\mu^a\varepsilon^j\bigr) \bigl(\mu^b\varepsilon^k\bigr) =\mu^{a+b}\varepsilon^{j+k}.$$

Products of convex combinations are convex combinations too. If

$$z=\sum_s u_s a_s,\qquad w=\sum_t v_t b_t,$$

where $a_s,b_t$ are orbit points and both coefficient lists are nonnegative and sum to one, then

$$zw=\sum_{s,t}u_sv_t\,a_sb_t.$$

The new coefficients are nonnegative and sum to one. Passing to limits handles the closed hull. Therefore $zw\in C$.

Fix any $z\in C$. We have proved $zC\subseteq C$. If $v_1,\ldots,v_k$ are its vertices, choose convex coefficients

$$zv_i=\sum_{j=1}^k a_{ij}v_j, \qquad a_{ij}\geq0,\qquad\sum_j a_{ij}=1.$$

These are the rows of a stochastic matrix $A$, and

$$Av=zv,\qquad v=(v_1,\ldots,v_k)^T.$$

The vector is nonzero because $1\in C$. Thus $z$ is an eigenvalue. If $k<n$, append a stochastic block. We have proved one entire inclusion in Theorem A:

$$\boxed{C\subseteq\mathcal K_n\quad\text{whenever }C\text{ has at most }n\text{ vertices}.}$$

This is stronger than checking the generator alone. The same polygon witnesses every convex combination of its points.

Where the return equations enter

The finite certificate explains the shape of the theorem's proof. The earlier work has already produced power relations with positive convex weights; the angular information tells us which root choices belong to the geometric configuration.

For the eight-state example from the equations post,

$$\lambda^2(\lambda^3-t)^2=(1-t)^2, \qquad0<t<1.$$

By the angular estimate, the selected configuration has direction

$$\frac13<\theta=\frac{\arg\lambda}{2\pi}<\frac38.$$

Taking the square root of the polynomial relation leaves a genuine choice:

$$\lambda^4=t\lambda\pm(1-t).$$

The sector rules out the plus sign. If the sign were positive, $\lambda^4$ would be a positive convex combination of $1$ and $\lambda$, so its argument would lie strictly between $0$ and $2\pi\theta$. But its principal direction is $4\theta-1$, and

$$\theta<4\theta-1<\frac12.$$

That is beyond the direction of $\lambda$, a contradiction. The correct closing relation is therefore

$$\boxed{\lambda^4=t\lambda+(1-t)(-1).}$$

Now include the half-turn copies:

$$C=\operatorname{conv}\{\pm1,\pm\lambda, \pm\lambda^2,\pm\lambda^3\}.$$

The boxed relation places $\lambda^4$ on $[-1,\lambda]$; its negative closes the other chain. Our finite certificate applies with $h=2$, $L=4$. This is a cyclic polygon with at most eight vertices, and it contains $\lambda$.

Figure 3. In this algebraic instance of the eight-state relation, four initial powers do not suffice. Adding their negatives supplies the missing point $-1$ and the edge containing $\lambda^4$. The two convex closing relations certify all subsequent powers.

The rotational factor $h\geq 1$ is an essential part of Karpelevich’s definition: the eight-state calculation above already needed $h=2$ so that $-1$ could supply the missing closing endpoint. Đoković later studied cyclic polygons systematically, connecting the $h=1$ case to polynomials with decreasing nonnegative coefficients in his streamlined proof of Karpelevich’s theorem.

The global input: cover the radial extrema

The eight-state calculation exhibits the mechanism. In the general construction, $\lambda$ may be a power of a new generator $\mu$. Extracting roots of the common-weight relations introduces roots of unity; these supply the finite rotational copies. Choosing those roots consistently and keeping the generating list within the vertex budget requires the general exponent and angle bookkeeping.

Theorem X supplies the result: a radial extremum new at order $n_*$, in the nonreal strict-contraction orientation treated by the proof, belongs to a cyclic polygon with at most $n_*$ vertices.

That is the global geometric input we use here. The general root-choice argument for both patterns is not reproduced by the worked example. Its angular hypotheses matter; an arbitrary root of one of the candidate equations is not being declared a boundary point.

Conjugation handles the opposite orientation. Unit-circle extrema are roots of unity and lie in their regular polygons. The real axis is covered by the cyclic segment $[-1,1]$. The remaining low-order nonreal case is covered by the regular triangle.

Why the order-three case needs only the triangle and the real segment

If a real $3\times3$ stochastic matrix has a nonreal eigenvalue $z=x+iy$, its eigenvalues are $1,z,\overline z$. Nonnegative diagonal entries give $1+2x=\operatorname{tr}A\geq0$, so $x\geq-1/2$.

Also,

$$\operatorname{tr}(A^2) =\sum_i a_{ii}^2+2\sum_{i<j}a_{ij}a_{ji} \geq\frac{(\operatorname{tr}A)^2}{3}.$$

Substituting the eigenvalues gives $y^2\leq(1-x)^2/3$. Together with $x\geq-1/2$ and $x\leq1$, these inequalities put $z$ in the triangle with vertices $1,e^{2\pi i/3},e^{4\pi i/3}$.

Every point of that triangle is realized by a convex combination of the identity and the two three-cycle permutation matrices. Real points in $[-1,1]$ are realized at order two. Thus the separate base cases are cyclic polygons too.

Fill the rays to obtain the whole region

Take any nonzero $z\in\mathcal K_n$. Compactness gives a farthest realizable point $\lambda$ on its ray, so

$$z=s\lambda,\qquad0<s\leq1.$$

Choose the least order $n_*\leq n$ realizing $\lambda$. It is a radial extremum at that order and is new there. The preceding boundary-covering result, including its elementary exceptions, places $\lambda$ in a cyclic polygon $C$ with at most $n$ vertices.

If the generator has $|\mu|<1$, its powers tend to zero, so $0\in C$. If $|\mu|=1$, a finite polygon requires a finite unit-circle orbit; every nontrivial such orbit also has zero in its convex hull. The exceptional singleton $\{1\}$ can use $[-1,1]$. Convexity now gives

$$[0,\lambda]\subseteq C,\qquad z\in C.$$

Figure 4. Theorem X supplies a polygon containing the radial extreme. Its convexity and the presence of zero fill the ray beneath that point. Different rays may use different cyclic polygons; the picture shows one such polygon.

Zero itself lies in $[-1,1]$. Combined with the inclusion proved in section 3, this proves the union description:

$$\boxed{\displaystyle \mathcal K_n= \bigcup_{\substack{C\ \text{cyclic polygon}\\ \#\operatorname{vertices}(C)\leq n}}C, \qquad n\geq2.}$$

The union need not be convex. Each individual member is a convex region of realizable eigenvalues; their outer envelope is the Karpelevich boundary.

What remains for the final stop

Using Theorem X, we now have a description of the whole region. Assigning every boundary arc its final equation is the remaining task.

That distinction is useful. Theorem A asks whether a point lies in at least one cyclic polygon with the allowed number of vertices. Theorem B asks which of the candidate curves form the outside of their union, including the pieces inherited from smaller orders.
