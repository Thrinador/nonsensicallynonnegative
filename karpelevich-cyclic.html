So far, our polygons have been witnesses. To prove that a particular number $\lambda$ is a stochastic eigenvalue, we find a polygon $Q$ with at most $n$ vertices such that $\lambda Q\subseteq Q$.

Karpelevich now turns that picture into a description of the region itself. He finds special polygons $C$ for which every point of $C$ is a stochastic eigenvalue, using $C$ as its own witness. His Theorem A says that these polygons, taken together, cover the whole Karpelevich region.

The extra property is simple to state:

$$z,w\in C\quad\Longrightarrow\quad zw\in C.$$

This chapter proves how the two common-weight return patterns produce these polygons, keeps their phases explicit, and counts their vertices. That construction completes the union description of the whole region.

What “cyclic” means here

Choose a complex number $\mu$ and a positive integer $h$, and write $\varepsilon=e^{2\pi i/h}$. Consider all the points

$$\mu^m\varepsilon^j,\qquad m=0,1,2,\ldots,\qquad j=0,\ldots,h-1.$$

Start with the powers of $\mu$, then include their $h$ rotated copies. When their closed convex hull is a finite polygon, call it $C(\mu,h)$. This is the power-generated, rotational meaning of cyclic polygon used in this post.

We count actual vertices of the hull. A list of eight generators might have fewer than eight vertices. Also, taking the first few powers does not automatically give the hull of the entire infinite orbit: the next power might fall outside.

The closed-hull convention includes real-axis segments. In the nondegenerate examples below it agrees with the ordinary convex hull of the infinite generating set. Throughout the union statement we take $n\geq2$.

Lemma 8.1One closing relation captures an infinite orbit

Here is a finite certificate. Form

$$P=\operatorname{conv}\{\mu^m\varepsilon^j: 0\leq m<L,\ 0\leq j<h\}.$$

Suppose the next power satisfies

$$\boxed{\mu^L\in P.}$$

Then $P=C(\mu,h)$, and it has at most $hL$ vertices.

To see this, multiplication by $\varepsilon$ permutes the generating list, so $\varepsilon P=P$. Multiplication by $\mu$ advances every generator to the next power. The only images needing a check are the terminal ones, $\varepsilon^j\mu^L$; they lie in $P$ by the boxed condition and rotational symmetry. Hence

$$\mu P\subseteq P.$$

Induction now puts every later power and every rotated copy inside $P$. The finite hull already contains the entire infinite orbit.

Our four-state example from the roadmap makes this concrete. Put

$$\mu=\frac{-1+i\sqrt{11}}4.$$

Direct calculation gives

$$\mu^4=\frac38+\frac58\mu.$$

Thus the next power lies on the segment joining $1$ to $\mu$. With $h=1$ and $L=4$,

$$C(\mu,1)=\operatorname{conv}\{1,\mu,\mu^2,\mu^3\}.$$

Figure 1. The amber point $\mu^4$ lies on $[1,\mu]$. Multiplication advances the first three generators and sends the fourth onto that edge. Every later power stays in the same quadrilateral.

Proposition 8.2Why every point in a cyclic polygon is realizable

Products of orbit points are orbit points:

$$\bigl(\mu^a\varepsilon^j\bigr) \bigl(\mu^b\varepsilon^k\bigr) =\mu^{a+b}\varepsilon^{j+k}.$$

Products of convex combinations are convex combinations too. If

$$z=\sum_s u_s a_s,\qquad w=\sum_t v_t b_t,$$

where $a_s,b_t$ are orbit points and both coefficient lists are nonnegative and sum to one, then

$$zw=\sum_{s,t}u_sv_t\,a_sb_t.$$

The new coefficients are nonnegative and sum to one. Passing to limits handles the closed hull. Therefore $zw\in C$.

Fix any $z\in C$. We have proved $zC\subseteq C$. If $v_1,\ldots,v_k$ are its vertices, choose convex coefficients

$$zv_i=\sum_{j=1}^k a_{ij}v_j, \qquad a_{ij}\geq0,\qquad\sum_j a_{ij}=1.$$

These are the rows of a stochastic matrix $A$, and

$$Av=zv,\qquad v=(v_1,\ldots,v_k)^T.$$

The vector is nonzero because $1\in C$. Thus $z$ is an eigenvalue. If $k<n$, append a stochastic block. We have proved one entire inclusion in Theorem A:

$$\boxed{C\subseteq\mathcal K_n\quad\text{whenever }C\text{ has at most }n\text{ vertices}.}$$

This is stronger than checking the generator alone. The same polygon witnesses every convex combination of its points.

Where the return equations enter

The finite certificate explains the shape of the theorem's proof. The earlier work has already produced power relations with positive convex weights; the angular information tells us which root choices belong to the geometric configuration.

For the eight-state example from the equations post,

$$\lambda^2(\lambda^3-t)^2=(1-t)^2, \qquad0<t<1.$$

By the angular estimate, the selected configuration has direction

$$\frac13<\theta=\frac{\arg\lambda}{2\pi}<\frac38.$$

Taking the square root of the polynomial relation leaves a genuine choice:

$$\lambda^4=t\lambda\pm(1-t).$$

The sector rules out the plus sign. If the sign were positive, $\lambda^4$ would be a positive convex combination of $1$ and $\lambda$, so its argument would lie strictly between $0$ and $2\pi\theta$. But its principal direction is $4\theta-1$, and

$$\theta<4\theta-1<\frac12.$$

That is beyond the direction of $\lambda$, a contradiction. The correct closing relation is therefore

$$\boxed{\lambda^4=t\lambda+(1-t)(-1).}$$

Now include the half-turn copies:

$$C=\operatorname{conv}\{\pm1,\pm\lambda, \pm\lambda^2,\pm\lambda^3\}.$$

The boxed relation places $\lambda^4$ on $[-1,\lambda]$; its negative closes the other chain. Our finite certificate applies with $h=2$, $L=4$. This is a cyclic polygon with at most eight vertices, and it contains $\lambda$.

Figure 3. In this algebraic instance of the eight-state relation, four initial powers do not suffice. Adding their negatives supplies the missing point $-1$ and the edge containing $\lambda^4$. The two convex closing relations certify all subsequent powers.

The half-turn supplies the missing endpoint in this particular eight-state certificate. That example alone does not exclude a different one-orbit certificate for the same point. The stronger claim that $h=1$ always suffices was disproved by Đoković with a counterexample at order six. Karpelevich’s definition allows the finite rotational factor used here.

Prove the covering of radial extrema

The constructionBézout lemmaForward patterngcd patternVertex count

We now prove the general construction behind Theorem X. The eight-state picture is one instance of it. The essential count will be

$$\boxed{\text{number of rotations}\;\times\;\text{number of initial powers}=n_*.}$$

Here $n_*$ is the least order realizing the radial extremum under consideration. Counting generators gives an upper bound on actual vertices; we will not assume that every generator is exposed.

What we may use from Stops 5 and 7

For a nonreal strict-contraction extremum new at order $n_*>3$, Stop 5 proves the common-weight relations, and Stop 7 identifies their exponents, including the single-contact case. After conjugating if necessary, one of the following holds, with $0<t<1$:

Return pattern Necessary relation Exact order count
Forward $\lambda^\tau(\lambda^\ell-t)^m=(1-t)^m$ $n_*=\ell m+\tau$, $0\leq\tau<\ell$
gcd $(\lambda^\ell-t)^m=(1-t)^m\lambda^\sigma$ $n_*=\ell m$, $0<\sigma<\ell$

In both rows $\ell,m$ are positive integers and $0<|\lambda|<1$. These are inputs from the preceding proofs, not consequences of the final boundary theorem.

We need only these algebraic relations for the covering construction. The angular information was needed to obtain the correct return description; we do not need to assume that a polynomial root continues to a particular endpoint as $t$ varies.

Lemma 8.3Keep the phase: a Bézout lemma

Suppose $\lambda,w\ne0$ and

$$\lambda^s=w^m,\qquad s\geq0,\quad m\geq1.$$

Put

$$\delta=\gcd(s,m),\qquad s'=s/\delta,\qquad m'=m/\delta.$$

Choose integers $u,v$ with $um'+vs'=1$, and define

$$\zeta=\lambda^{s'}w^{-m'},\qquad \mu=\lambda^u w^v.$$

Negative integer powers are legitimate because both numbers are nonzero. Direct calculation gives

$$\boxed{\zeta^\delta=1,\qquad \lambda=\mu^{m'}\zeta^v,\qquad w=\mu^{s'}\zeta^{-u}.}$$

Indeed, the first identity is $\lambda^s/w^m=1$. Since $w^{m'}=\lambda^{s'}\zeta^{-1}$, we have

$$\mu^{m'}=\lambda^{um'}(w^{m'})^v =\lambda^{um'+vs'}\zeta^{-v} =\lambda\zeta^{-v}.$$

Likewise $\lambda^{s'}=w^{m'}\zeta$ gives $\mu^{s'}=w\zeta^u$. This proves all three identities without choosing a multivalued complex logarithm.

The moduli also behave correctly. From $|w|^m=|\lambda|^s$ and the Bézout identity,

$$|\mu|=|\lambda|^{u+vs/m}=|\lambda|^{1/m'}<1.$$

The point is to retain the root-of-unity factor on $\lambda$. We need $\lambda$ to be a rotated power of $\mu$; we do not need it to be an unrotated power. All the phases lie in the group of $\delta$th roots of unity.

The case $s=0$ is included: $\delta=m$, $s'=0$, $m'=1$, and we can take $u=1,v=0$. No division by $s$ occurs.

The forward return pattern

Set

$$w=\frac{1-t}{\lambda^\ell-t}.$$

The denominator is nonzero by the forward equation, and that equation says $\lambda^\tau=w^m$. Apply the lemma with $s=\tau$. Thus

$$\delta=\gcd(\tau,m),\quad \tau'=\tau/\delta,\quad m'=m/\delta,\quad um'+v\tau'=1.$$

The definition of $w$ gives a two-term convex relation:

$$\lambda^\ell w=t w+(1-t).$$

Substitute $\lambda=\mu^{m'}\zeta^v$ and $w=\mu^{\tau'}\zeta^{-u}$, then divide by $\zeta^{\ell v-u}$. We obtain

$$\boxed{\mu^L =t\mu^{\tau'}\zeta^{-\ell v} +(1-t)\zeta^{u-\ell v},\qquad L=\ell m'+\tau'=n_*/\delta.}$$

Both terms on the right are among the $\delta$ rotated copies of the first $L$ powers: $0\leq\tau'<L$, and $\zeta$ is a $\delta$th root of unity. Therefore $\mu^L$ lies in their convex hull. This is exactly the finite closing certificate from section 2.

The gcd return pattern

This time set

$$w=\frac{\lambda^\ell-t}{1-t}.$$

The gcd equation says $\lambda^\sigma=w^m$. Apply the same lemma with $s=\sigma$, writing

$$\delta=\gcd(\sigma,m),\quad \sigma'=\sigma/\delta,\quad m'=m/\delta,\quad um'+v\sigma'=1.$$

Now the convex relation is $\lambda^\ell=t+(1-t)w$. Substitution and division by $\zeta^{\ell v}$ give

$$\boxed{\mu^L =t\zeta^{-\ell v} +(1-t)\mu^{\sigma'}\zeta^{-u-\ell v},\qquad L=\ell m'=n_*/\delta.}$$

Here $0<\sigma'<L$, so once again both endpoints are rotated initial powers. The same finite closing certificate applies.

The count is the number of generators

In either pattern, let $\varepsilon=e^{2\pi i/\delta}$ and form

$$P=\operatorname{conv}\{\mu^j\varepsilon^a: 0\leq j<L,\ 0\leq a<\delta\}.$$

Since $\zeta^\delta=1$, every phase in the boxed relations is some power of $\varepsilon$. Hence $\mu^L\in P$, and section 2 proves

$$P=C(\mu,\delta),\qquad \#\operatorname{vertices}(P)\leq\delta L=n_*.$$

The lemma also gives $\lambda=\mu^{m'}\zeta^v\in C(\mu,\delta)$. This remains true even if that particular power is beyond the initial list, because the closing certificate already includes the entire infinite orbit. Finally, $|\mu|<1$ implies $0\in P$.

This proves the general construction, with both the phase and the vertex budget accounted for. A root of either return equation can be covered this way without being a boundary point. Deciding which roots actually trace the boundary remains the task of Stop 9.

A root phase that cannot be dropped

Take the forward data $n_*=14$, $\ell=3$, $m=4$, $\tau=2$. The necessary relation is

$$\lambda^2(\lambda^3-t)^4=(1-t)^4.$$

Here $\delta=2$, $m'=2$, $\tau'=1$. Choose $u=0,v=1$, so

$$\mu=w=\frac{1-t}{\lambda^3-t},\qquad \zeta=\lambda/\mu^2\in\{1,-1\}.$$

The closing relation becomes

$$\boxed{\mu^7=\zeta\bigl(t\mu+(1-t)\bigr),\qquad \lambda=\zeta\mu^2.}$$

Thus two rotations and seven initial powers suffice: at most fourteen vertices. If $\zeta=-1$, the covering polygon contains $\lambda=-\mu^2$ on the rotated chain. Requiring $\lambda=\mu^2$ would discard exactly the phase that makes this construction work.

Theorem 8.4Finish the extremal covering Karpelevich §5, Thm. X

For the conjugate orientation, conjugate the constructed polygon. Indeed, $\overline{C(\mu,h)}=C(\overline\mu,h)$, since conjugation permutes the $h$th roots of unity. The vertex count is unchanged.

For a unit-circle stochastic eigenvalue, the root-of-unity order is at most the matrix order. Here is a short proof that also covers reducible matrices. If $Av=\lambda v$ and $|\lambda|=1$, choose $i$ with $|v_i|=\max_j|v_j|>0$. Equality in

$$|\lambda v_i|=\left|\sum_j a_{ij}v_j\right| \leq\sum_j a_{ij}|v_j|\leq|v_i|$$

forces $v_j=\lambda v_i$ whenever $a_{ij}>0$. Every such successor again has maximal modulus. Following positive entries eventually yields a directed cycle of some length $d\leq n_*$. Along it, $v_i=\lambda^d v_i$, so $\lambda^d=1$. The regular $d$-gon $C(1,d)$ contains $\lambda$; if $\lambda=1$, use $[-1,1]$ instead so the chosen set also contains zero.

Real extrema are covered by the cyclic segment $[-1,1]=C(1,2)$. The low-order nonreal case is covered by the regular triangle, as the calculation below shows. Consequently every radial extremum is contained, together with zero, in a cyclic polygon whose vertex count is within the allowed order. For a real or unit extremum of least order one, the segment uses two vertices, which is allowed throughout our union statement $n\geq2$.

Why the order-three case needs only the triangle and the real segment

If a real $3\times3$ stochastic matrix has a nonreal eigenvalue $z=x+iy$, its eigenvalues are $1,z,\overline z$. Nonnegative diagonal entries give $1+2x=\operatorname{tr}A\geq0$, so $x\geq-1/2$.

Also,

$$\operatorname{tr}(A^2) =\sum_i a_{ii}^2+2\sum_{i<j}a_{ij}a_{ji} \geq\frac{(\operatorname{tr}A)^2}{3}.$$

Substituting the eigenvalues gives $y^2\leq(1-x)^2/3$. Together with $x\geq-1/2$ and $x\leq1$, these inequalities put $z$ in the triangle with vertices $1,e^{2\pi i/3},e^{4\pi i/3}$.

Every point of that triangle is realized by a convex combination of the identity and the two three-cycle permutation matrices. Real points in $[-1,1]$ are realized at order two. Thus the separate base cases are cyclic polygons too.

Theorem AFill the rays to obtain the whole region Karpelevich 1951, Thm. A

Take any nonzero $z\in\mathcal K_n$. Compactness gives a farthest realizable point $\lambda$ on its ray, so

$$z=s\lambda,\qquad0<s\leq1.$$

Choose the least order $n_*\leq n$ realizing $\lambda$. It is a radial extremum at that order and is new there. The preceding boundary-covering result, including its elementary exceptions, places $\lambda$ in a cyclic polygon $C$ with at most $n$ vertices.

The construction just proved gives $|\mu|<1$ for every strict-contraction case, so $0\in C$. Each nontrivial regular polygon also contains zero, since the sum of its vertices is zero; the real segment $[-1,1]$ does too. We use this segment for $\lambda=1$, including when its least realizing order is one. It has two vertices, which is allowed because $n\geq2$. Thus the covering polygon can always be chosen to contain both zero and $\lambda$. Convexity gives

$$[0,\lambda]\subseteq C,\qquad z\in C.$$

Figure 5. The proved cyclic construction supplies a polygon containing the radial extreme. Its convexity and the presence of zero fill the ray beneath that point. Different rays may use different cyclic polygons; the picture shows one such polygon.

Zero itself lies in $[-1,1]$. Combined with the inclusion proved in section 3, this proves the union description:

$$\boxed{\displaystyle \mathcal K_n= \bigcup_{\substack{C\ \text{cyclic polygon}\\ \#\operatorname{vertices}(C)\leq n}}C, \qquad n\geq2.}$$

The union need not be convex. Each individual member is a convex region of realizable eigenvalues; their outer envelope is the Karpelevich boundary.

What remains for the final stop

With Theorem X proved from the return relations, we now have a description of the whole region. Assigning every boundary arc its final equation is the remaining task.

That distinction is useful. Theorem A asks whether a point lies in at least one cyclic polygon with the allowed number of vertices. Theorem B asks which of the candidate curves form the outside of their union, including the pieces inherited from smaller orders.
