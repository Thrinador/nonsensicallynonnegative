We have equations for the boundary candidates. An equation can have many complex roots, and its endpoint equations can have many roots of unity. We still need to locate the relevant roots in the plane.

Karpelevich gets that information from angles in the invariant polygon. The integer congruences already controlling the vertex chains become determinant-one identities between rational angles. Those identities identify Farey neighbors.

This post proves the Farey arithmetic and the angular bounds in both branches, then closes the single-contact configuration gap by an invariant slide. We use the eight-state equation to keep the sector labels and the missing-case exclusion concrete.

The unit circle supplies rational landmarks

Write a direction in turns:

$$\theta=\frac{\arg\lambda}{2\pi},\qquad \lambda=|\lambda|e^{2\pi i\theta}.$$

From the polygon correspondence, the unit-circle points in $\mathcal K_n$ are exactly the roots of unity of order at most $n$. Thus their directions are the reduced fractions

$$\frac ab,\qquad 0\leq a\leq b,\qquad \gcd(a,b)=1,\qquad b\leq n.$$

In increasing order, these form the Farey sequence $F_n$. The fractions $0/1$ and $1/1$ represent the same point $1$ on the circle; keep both when discussing the interval $[0,1]$, and identify them when drawing the circle.

For example, the upper-half directions in $F_7$ are

$$0,\ \frac17,\ \frac16,\ \frac15,\ \frac14,\ \frac27,\ \frac13,\ \frac25,\ \frac37,\ \frac12.$$

Figure 1. Consecutive fractions $1/3$ and $2/5$ give directions $120^\circ$ and $144^\circ$. At order seven, no other unit-circle landmark lies between them. The picture marks directions and landmarks; it does not draw a boundary arc.

Lemma 7.1What makes two fractions neighbors?

Two reduced fractions $a/b<c/d$ are consecutive in $F_n$ exactly when

$$\boxed{bc-ad=1,\qquad b+d>n.}$$

The determinant condition makes them a particularly close rational pair. The denominator condition says their gap contains no allowable new landmark at this order.

Here is the useful calculation. Suppose $bc-ad=1$ and a fraction $u/v$ lies strictly between them. The two integers

$$bu-av\quad\text{and}\quad cv-du$$

are positive. Moreover,

$$\boxed{v=b(cv-du)+d(bu-av)\geq b+d.}$$

Every fraction between the pair therefore needs denominator at least $b+d$.

The bound is attained by the mediant:

$$\frac{a+c}{b+d}.$$

It is reduced, because a common divisor of its numerator and denominator would divide $b(a+c)-a(b+d)=1$. It is also the unique fraction in the gap with the minimum denominator: equality in the boxed identity forces both positive integers to be one.

Why the determinant-one condition is necessary too

The preceding argument proves that determinant one together with $b+d>n$ is sufficient. To find the actual predecessor of a reduced $c/d\in F_n$ with $0<c/d<1$, use the Euclidean algorithm to solve

$$cb\equiv1\pmod d.$$

Choose the largest positive solution $b\leq n$, and put $a=(cb-1)/d$. Then $a/b$ is reduced, lies below $c/d$, and satisfies $bc-ad=1$. Maximality of $b$ gives $b+d>n$.

The denominator bound now proves that no fraction of $F_n$ lies between these two. Thus this construction finds the predecessor, and every neighboring pair has determinant one. The endpoint $1/1$ has predecessor $(n-1)/n$, which satisfies the same criterion.

The angular width of a neighboring gap is consequently exact:

$$\frac cd-\frac ab=\frac1{bd},\qquad \Delta\arg=\frac{2\pi}{bd}.$$

A new order inserts a mediant

The pair $1/3<2/5$ is neighboring at order seven:

$$3\cdot2-1\cdot5=1,\qquad3+5=8>7.$$

Its first new fraction is $3/8$. It appears at order eight, splitting the angular gap into two neighboring gaps:

$$\frac13<\frac38<\frac25.$$

Figure 2. The old gap has width $24^\circ$. Its new pieces have widths $15^\circ$ and $9^\circ$. The mediant is not generally the angular midpoint.

The neighbor relation depends on the matrix order. A pair stays neighboring through order $b+d-1$ and separates when order $b+d$ admits its mediant. This describes the changing angular labels. How the actual boundary curves change is part of the later induction.

The polygon’s angle bounds produce these exact neighbors

Return to a nonreal radial extremum new at order $n>3$, with $0<|\lambda|<1$. Choose the contact orientation from the earlier posts, with $1\leq k<n$, and let $k$ be its cyclic shift. Initially the contact theorem gives the coarse sector

$$\frac{k-1}{n}\leq\theta\leq\frac{k}{n}.$$

We now prove the refinements corresponding to Karpelevich’s angular estimates, Theorems VIII–IX. Put $g=\gcd(k,n)$. Choose the unique integers $f,b$ satisfying

$$\boxed{kb-nf=g,\qquad0<n-b<n/g.}$$

Then the chosen orientation lies in the open sector

$$\boxed{\frac fb<\theta<\frac kn.}$$

The next section proves this refinement in both return branches, including the singleton case. Its Farey meaning follows immediately. Reduce $k/n$ to $c/d$, where $c=k/g$ and $d=n/g$. Dividing the determinant identity by $g$ gives

$$cb-df=1,$$

and the bound on $b$ gives $b+d>n$. Thus $f/b$ and $c/d$ are neighbors in $F_n$.

$7$ $3$ $3\cdot5-7\cdot2=1$ $2/5<\theta<3/7$

Conjugation reflects a sector and reverses its endpoints:

$$\left(\frac ab,\frac cd\right)\longmapsto\left(1-\frac cd,\ 1-\frac ab\right).$$

The determinant and denominator conditions are preserved, so the same labeling works in the opposite orientation.

Lemma 7.2Lift the vertex angles before taking powers

Rotate and scale the polygon so that $x_0=1$. Write its vertex directions in turns as real numbers

$$\Phi_0=0<\Phi_1<\cdots<\Phi_{n-1}<1, \qquad\Phi_{i+n}=\Phi_i+1.$$

Put $\gamma_i=\Phi_i-\Phi_{i-1}$. Since the origin is interior to a genuine convex polygon,

$$0<\gamma_i<\frac12.$$

The half-open contact ordering proved in Stop 2 gives a consistent lift:

$$\Phi_{i+k-1}<\Phi_i+\theta\leq\Phi_{i+k}.$$

The right equality holds for a vertex contact; both inequalities are strict for an edge contact. To see why the same lift works at every vertex, radial projection identifies the polygon boundary with the circle. Each half-open edge contains exactly one image vertex, in cyclic order. Starting with the edge containing $\lambda x_0$, the next image therefore uses the next edge, with its integer index retained rather than reduced modulo $n$.

Along a deterministic chain, the equalities add. If its first $h-1$ images are vertices and its $h$th image is an interior edge contact, then

$$\boxed{\Phi_{i+hk-1}<\Phi_i+h\theta<\Phi_{i+hk}.}$$

This is an inequality of real numbers. It retains every complete turn made by the path. We will use it when a power has argument close to a multiple of $2\pi$, where a principal argument alone would lose essential information.

Theorem 7.3Forward branch: the power calculation Karpelevich §5, Thm. VIII

Here is a complete derivation for the forward branch from the rigidity post, in the standard notation: $\ell$ is the chain length, $m$ the number of repeated chains, and $\tau$ the tail. Its relations include

$$\lambda^\ell x_0=\alpha x_0+\beta x_1,\qquad\alpha,\beta>0,\quad\alpha+\beta=1,$$

and $n=\ell m+\tau$ with $m\geq2$, $\tau\geq0$. In particular $\ell\leq n/2$. The arithmetic gives

$$k\ell-fn=1.$$

Since $x_0,x_1$ are consecutive counterclockwise vertices and the origin is interior, their angular separation is strictly between zero and $\pi$. Dividing the contact equation by $x_0$ therefore gives

$$0<\operatorname{Arg}(\lambda^\ell)<\pi.$$

We must relate this principal argument to $\ell\theta$. The coarse sector and the integer identity imply

$$\frac{1-\ell}{n}\leq \ell\theta-f\leq\frac1n.$$

Because $\ell\leq n/2$ and $n>3$, this whole interval lies inside $(-1/2,1/2)$. There is no ambiguity about the principal turn:

$$\frac{\operatorname{Arg}(\lambda^\ell)}{2\pi}=\ell\theta-f.$$

Its positivity forces $\theta>f/\ell$. Also $\theta<k/n$: on the endpoint ray the root $e^{2\pi ik/n}$ is realizable, so a strict contraction there cannot be radially extremal. We obtain

$$\boxed{\frac{f}{\ell}<\theta<\frac kn.}$$

This is precisely the Farey pair with $k\ell-fn=1$. For the eight-state example, $\ell=3$, $f=1$, and $k/n=3/8$.

Theorem 7.4The gcd branch: sum the angular deficits Karpelevich §5, Thm. IX

Suppose the reduced contact block has length $\varphi=g=\gcd(n,k)$. This includes the single-contact case $g=1$. Put

$$\ell=\frac ng,\qquad a=\frac kg.$$

There is one contact in each index cycle, so the return from $x_i$ reaches the edge $[x_{i-1},x_i]$ after $\ell$ steps. Its unreduced index advance is $\ell k=an$. The lifted inequality therefore says

$$\Phi_{i-1}+a<\Phi_i+\ell\theta<\Phi_i+a, \qquad 1\leq i\leq g.$$

Define the angular deficit $\delta=a-\ell\theta$. The preceding inequalities are exactly

$$\boxed{0<\delta<\gamma_i\quad(1\leq i\leq g).}$$

The return power falls short of $a$ complete turns by less than the angular gap of its target edge.

Let $\sigma$ be the closing time from Stop 4:

$$\lambda^\sigma x_g=x_0,\qquad k\sigma\equiv-g\pmod n,\qquad0<\sigma<\ell.$$

Every step on this closing path is a vertex step. Its full index advance gives

$$\Phi_g+\sigma\theta =\Phi_0+\frac{g+k\sigma}{n}.$$

Consequently, if $\Gamma=\gamma_1+\cdots+\gamma_g=\Phi_g-\Phi_0$, then

$$\Gamma=\frac{g+k\sigma}{n}-\sigma\theta, \qquad g\delta=k-n\theta<\Gamma.$$

Set

$$b=n-\sigma,\qquad f=\frac{kb-g}{n}.$$

The congruence makes $f$ an integer. Subtracting the last two expressions gives the useful exact identity

$$\boxed{\Gamma-g\delta=b\theta-f>0.}$$

Thus $\theta>f/b$. Positivity of $\delta$ also gives $\theta<a/\ell=k/n$. We have proved the angular estimate in this branch:

$$\boxed{\frac fb<\theta<\frac kn, \qquad kb-nf=g,\qquad0<n-b<n/g.}$$

Dividing the determinant identity by $g$ gives

$$ab-\ell f=1,\qquad b+\ell>n.$$

Both endpoint fractions are reduced, and the Farey criterion proved earlier shows that $f/b$ and $a/\ell$ are neighbors in $F_n$. This proves the needed gcd-branch refinement directly. Equal edge angles or a prior identification of the boundary arcs are not required.

For $n=8$, $k=2$, we have $g=2$, $\ell=4$, $\sigma=3$, $b=5$, and $f=1$. The two inequalities become

$$\delta=1-4\theta>0, \qquad2\delta<\Gamma=1-3\theta.$$

They give $\theta<1/4$ and $\theta>1/5$, respectively. The same computation with $g=1$ supplies the angular sector for a singleton contact, before we decide which singleton patterns can be extremal.

Lemma 7.5The single-contact case, without assuming the boundary theorem

The remaining configuration has $g=1$ and just one edge contact. Label its target edge $[x_0,x_1]$, so

$$\xi_1=a x_0+(1-a)x_1,\qquad0<a<1.$$

All other target coefficients alpha are zero: those images are vertex contacts. The angular argument above identifies the Farey predecessor $f/b$ of $k/n$, with

$$kb-nf=1,\qquad1\leq b<n.$$

We will prove that a singleton can be extremal only if $b>n/2$. This settles the case previously left to the final theorem.

Enlarge the marked window

Put

$$m=\left\lfloor\frac nb\right\rfloor,\qquad \tau=n-bm,\qquad0\leq\tau<b.$$

Mark the vertices $x_1,\ldots,x_m$. These extra marks cut the deterministic paths into shorter chains; they do not declare new edge contacts. Only mark $1$ carries the positive coefficient $a$. At the added marks $2,\ldots,m$, the alpha coefficient is zero and the beta coefficient is one.

Here is a complete check that this is a valid return decomposition. Assign height $b$ to bases $1,\ldots,m-1$, height $b+\tau$ to base $m$, and return each base to its successor, with $m$ returning to $1$. The identities

$$bk\equiv1\pmod n,\qquad \tau k\equiv-m\pmod n,\qquad bm+\tau=n$$

make every step in the concatenated chains add $k$ modulo $n$, including each last step into the next base. The concatenation is one cycle of $n$ positions. Since $\gcd(k,n)=1$, adding $k$ has period exactly $n$, so these positions visit all vertex indices once, with no repetition.

No intermediate chain position can be another marked base: that would duplicate the same index at its base position. Thus the displayed heights really are first-return times to the marked window. Before a return, every image is a vertex because the sole genuine edge-contact target, label $1$, lies inside the window.

The last chain reaches label $0$ after $\tau$ steps, because $m+\tau k\equiv0\pmod n$. Splitting it there gives the relations

$$\begin{aligned} \lambda^b x_0&=a x_0+(1-a)x_1,\\ \lambda^b x_{j-1}&=x_j,&&2\leq j\leq m,\\ \lambda^\tau x_m&=x_0. \end{aligned}$$

When $\tau=0$, the last identity means $x_m=x_0$; it is the full-window identification rather than an extra vertex.

The same partition works whenever all genuine contact targets lie in the marked window: before the first return, no such target is encountered. The singleton assumption only specifies which of the window coefficients are zero. We will use this observation again in the exponent dictionary.

Proof figure B. At n = 8, k = 3, adding base 2 splits the singleton chain into return lengths three and five. The new mark has alpha coefficient zero; the contact at ξ₁ has positive alpha. These are the exact index relations used by the exclusion proof.

A positive contact beside a zero mark is impossible

Suppose $m\geq2$. Write

$$A=x_0,\qquad B=x_1,\qquad C=x_2,\qquad T=\lambda^b.$$

The first two return relations are

$$TA=aA+(1-a)B,\qquad TB=C.$$

This is precisely the zero-weight comparison from Stop 5. For completeness, move

$$B_s=B+s(B-A)$$

and propagate that motion along the entire chain generated by $B$. Keep every other marked chain fixed. Choose $-a<s<0$, with magnitude small enough to preserve the polygon's strict convexity.

The fixed image still lies on its new edge:

$$TA=\frac{a+s}{1+s}A+\frac{1-a}{1+s}B_s.$$

Both coefficients are positive. The moved terminal image has the exact expression

$$\boxed{TB_s= \frac{-s(a+s)}{1+s}A +\frac{-s(1-a)}{1+s}B_s +(1+s)C.}$$

These three coefficients sum to one and are strictly positive. Hence $TB_s$ is interior to the nondegenerate triangle with vertices $A,B_s,C$, and therefore interior to the moved polygon.

The return decomposition makes the global check explicit. Within the moved chain each image is its next moved vertex. Its last image is $TB_s=\lambda(\lambda^{b-1}B_s)$, the image of its last moved vertex. The fixed last chain still returns to $TA$, now on $[A,B_s]$. Every other terminal image and its target endpoints are fixed. These exhaust the vertex images. Thus the moved polygon remains invariant and has an interior image of a vertex, contrary to Stop 2.

Therefore $m\geq2$ is impossible. A singleton extremum must satisfy

$$\boxed{m=1,\qquad b>n/2.}$$

With one marked base, elimination now gives

$$\boxed{\lambda^{n-b}(\lambda^b-a)=1-a.}$$

This is Family I with $\ell=b$, $m=1$, and $\tau=n-b<b$. The raw singleton relation

$$\lambda^n-t=(1-t)\lambda^{n-b}$$

is the same equation after $a=1-t$. No selection between two different outer candidates is being assumed.

The eight-state case that exposed the gap

For $n=8$, $k=3$, the inverse is $b=3$, so $m=2$ and $\tau=2$. Marking bases $1,2$ gives

$$\lambda^3x_0=a x_0+(1-a)x_1,\qquad \lambda^3x_1=x_2,\qquad\lambda^2x_2=x_0.$$

The positive first contact and zero second mark admit the slide just proved. The singleton pattern therefore cannot represent a new radial extremum in the sector $(1/3,3/8)$.

The surviving repeated-chain pattern instead has the necessary equation

$$\lambda^2(\lambda^3-t)^2=(1-t)^2.$$

For comparison, at $n=7$, $k=3$, the inverse is $b=5$, so $m=1$. A singleton is not excluded by this test, and its necessary equation is

$$\lambda^2(\lambda^5-t)=1-t.$$

We have closed the single-contact configuration gap using return arithmetic and an invariant variation. Existence and final selection of the outer root branches still belong to the later assembly; this argument does not declare every polynomial root a boundary point.

Theorem 7.6The angular sector fixes the exponents Karpelevich §5, pp. 380–381

We can now identify the canonical equation for every nonreal radial extremum new at order $n>3$, including the single-contact case. Let its chosen shift be $k$, put $g=\gcd(n,k)$, and take the Farey sector just proved:

$$\frac fb<\theta<\frac kn,\qquad kb-nf=g,\qquad0<n-b<n/g.$$

Reduce $k/n$ before comparing denominators. Write $\ell,s$ for the smaller and larger Farey denominators and $m=\lfloor n/\ell\rfloor$.

Coprime shift: the forward family includes the singleton

If $g=1$, then $k/n$ is already reduced, so $s=n$ and $\ell=b$. The identity $kb-nf=1$ makes $b$ the inverse of $k$ modulo $n$.

For a singleton, the preceding proof gives $m=1$ and the forward relation directly. For a block with $\varphi>1$, Stop 5 gives $n=b\varphi+\tau_0$ with $\tau_0\geq0$, so $\varphi\leq\lfloor n/b\rfloor=m$. All genuine contact targets therefore lie in the enlarged window $1,\ldots,m$.

The inverse-window partition proved above applies to that window. Its return relations have chain length $b$ and closing tail $\tau=n-bm$. The neighboring-weight argument from Stop 5 forces every window alpha coefficient to agree, including any zero coefficients at added marks. At least one coefficient is positive, so there can be no added zero marks: $\varphi=m$.

Consequently, in every coprime-shift case,

$$\boxed{\ell=b,\quad s=n,\quad m=\lfloor n/\ell\rfloor,\quad\tau=n-\ell m,\quad0\leq\tau<\ell,}$$

and the common-weight relation is

$$\boxed{\lambda^\tau(\lambda^\ell-t)^m=(1-t)^m.}$$

Here $t$ is the alpha coefficient, the weight on the chain's own generator in the forward relations. This argument also proves the floor formula without assuming it from the final arc theorem.

Noncoprime shift: the gcd family

If $g\geq2$, Stop 5 gives $\varphi=g$ and $g$ chains of length $\ell=n/g$. The closing time in the angular proof is $\sigma=n-b$, with $0<\sigma<\ell$.

Since $b>n-\ell=\ell(g-1)\geq\ell$, the smaller Farey denominator is $\ell$ and the larger is $s=b$. Hence

$$\boxed{m=g=\lfloor n/\ell\rfloor,\qquad \ell m=n,\qquad\sigma=\ell m-s=n-b.}$$

The common-weight relation from Stop 5 is

$$\boxed{(\lambda^\ell-t)^m=(1-t)^m\lambda^\sigma.}$$

Here $t$ is the beta coefficient, the weight on the chain's own generator in the gcd relations. The two branches use opposite edges, which accounts for the different coefficient names.

Both are reductions of the unified algebraic equation

$$\lambda^s(\lambda^\ell-t)^m=(1-t)^m\lambda^{\ell m}.$$

This identifies the exponents in Ito's formulation by contact geometry, angular lifts, and return arithmetic. It does not import that formulation's boundary conclusion. The identities involving $n$ above concern new order-$n$ extrema; inherited arcs are handled at their smaller realizing order in Stop 9.

For the strict-contraction extrema considered here, $0<t<1$. The values zero and one occur as limiting parameters at the unit-circle landmarks.

The labels distinguish the endpoint roots

Our eight-state relations produced

$$\lambda^2(\lambda^3-t)^2=(1-t)^2.$$

The corresponding sector is $1/3<\theta<3/8$. At the parameter endpoints, the polynomial factors exactly:

An arc in this sector joining unit-circle landmarks must use those endpoint labels. The argument has identified their locations exactly. It has not certified every root of the equation, or every root path inside an angular sector, as an outer-boundary arc.

That final selection belongs to the induction on the order. The same distinction applies to the mediant picture: inserting a rational landmark organizes the sectors; establishing the new and inherited boundary pieces requires the remaining proof.
