We have reached the last comparison. The contact equations produce algebraic candidates. The angular estimates place them between the right roots of unity. The cyclic polygons describe the whole eigenvalue region. What remains is to decide which pieces form its boundary at each order.

The regions are nested:

$$\mathcal K_{n-1}\subseteq\mathcal K_n.$$

An old boundary point stays realizable. It may remain on the outside, or the larger order may put new realizable points beyond it. Karpelevich's final induction separates those possibilities.

The answer involves a surprisingly simple arithmetic test. The geometric reason behind it is that a polygon whose image vertices all lie strictly inside has room to realize nearby multipliers.

Nested regions do not have nested boundaries

Write directions in turns, and let

$$R_n(\theta)=\max\{r\geq0:re^{2\pi i\theta}\in\mathcal K_n\}.$$

Compactness makes this maximum exist. The invariant-polygon correspondence gives the radial segment beneath it. Nesting tells us $R_n(\theta)\geq R_{n-1}(\theta)$; it does not decide whether equality holds.

Let $\Gamma_n$ denote this radial outer frontier. We will work in open angular sectors between the unit-circle landmarks, treating the landmarks and the real-axis endpoints separately.

Our running example is the sector

$$\frac14<\theta<\frac13,$$

between $i$ and $\omega=e^{2\pi i/3}$. Its order-four arc survives unchanged at order five. At order six it moves outward, although its two endpoint labels stay the same.

This cannot be explained by inserting a new unit-circle landmark: the first fraction between $1/4$ and $1/3$ has denominator $4+3=7$.

Proposition 9.1The final arithmetic test

Take neighboring reduced fractions $a/b<c/d$ in the Farey sequence $F_n$. From stop 7,

$$bc-ad=1,\qquad b+d>n.$$

The final induction gives the following rule for the open arc in that sector, with the order-four case supplied as the base:

Denominators at order $n$ What happens to the arc
Neither $b$ nor $d$ divides $n$ It is inherited from order $n-1$.
At least one of $b,d$ divides $n$ Its open outer arc is new at order $n$.

“New” here means outside $\mathcal K_{n-1}$. It does not require new endpoint landmarks.

There are two ways the second row can occur. A denominator equal to $n$ introduces a new primitive $n$th root. A proper divisor of $n$ is an existing endpoint whose neighboring arc can change when the order increases.

The elementary identity behind the bookkeeping is

$$\left\lfloor\frac n b\right\rfloor= \left\lfloor\frac{n-1}{b}\right\rfloor \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad b\nmid n.$$

If neither denominator divides $n$, both already occur in $F_{n-1}$, the pair is still neighboring there, and the relevant division quotients remain unchanged. This preserves the inductive equation data. The winding and comparison arguments below prove that the inherited curve remains outermost.

Lemma 9.2Strictly interior images exclude a boundary point

Here is the local lemma doing the decisive work.

Suppose $Q$ is a genuine two-dimensional convex polygon with vertices $v_1,\ldots,v_k$, where $k\leq n$, and every $\mu v_j$ lies in its interior. Put

$$\delta=\min_j\operatorname{dist}(\mu v_j,\partial Q)>0, \qquad M=\max_j|v_j|>0.$$

If $|\nu-\mu|<\delta/M$, then

$$|\nu v_j-\mu v_j|\leq|\nu-\mu|M<\delta.$$

Every $\nu v_j$ is still inside $Q$. Convexity gives $\nu Q\subseteq Q$, so the polygon correspondence realizes $\nu$ at order at most $n$. Consequently,

$$\boxed{B(\mu,\delta/M)\subseteq\mathcal K_n.}$$

The point $\mu$ is an interior point of the eigenvalue region. In particular, if $\mu\ne0$, a small radial expansion $(1+\varepsilon)\mu$ is realizable. Thus $\mu$ cannot remain on the radial outer frontier.

One interior image would not suffice for this lemma: the positive minimum requires all image vertices to be strictly interior. This differs from the variation contradictions used earlier for a new extremum, where the contact theorem already required every image vertex to be on the contour.

An exact old point that order six buries

Return to

$$\lambda_0=\frac{-1+i\sqrt{11}}4.$$

Its four-state closing relation is

$$\lambda_0^4=\frac38+\frac58\lambda_0.$$

At order six, use the regular hexagon

$$Q=\operatorname{conv}\{e^{2\pi ij/6}:0\leq j<6\}.$$

This hexagon is described by

$$|y|\leq\frac{\sqrt3}{2},\qquad |x|+\frac{|y|}{\sqrt3}\leq1.$$

For $\lambda_0$, both inequalities are strict: $\sqrt{11}/4<\sqrt3/2$ and $(1+\sqrt{11/3})/4<1$. Rotational symmetry places every $\lambda_0v_j$ strictly inside $Q$. The lemma therefore proves that $\lambda_0$ is interior to $\mathcal K_6$.

We can even exhibit an exact outward move:

$$\nu=\frac{33}{32}\lambda_0\in\mathcal K_6.$$

For this point, the first hexagon inequality follows from

$$33^2\cdot11=11979<12288=64^2\cdot3.$$

For the other inequality, use $\sqrt{11/3}<2$ to obtain $33(1+\sqrt{11/3})/128<99/128<1$. Again all six image vertices are strictly interior.

Compare every old arc with the new candidates

We will prove the general exclusion used in Karpelevich's final induction. The construction has three stages: define a realizable candidate on each ray; compare the candidates at successive orders; then use the larger candidate's polygon to put every old image strictly inside.

The earlier stops provide three inputs, all proved there: the polygon–matrix correspondence; the common-weight return relations and their angular lifts; and explicit stochastic realizations of the return polynomials. The comparisons below prove which realizable candidates are outermost.

Proposition 9.3One scalar equation selects the intended branch

Take a Farey interval and, if necessary, conjugate so that its smaller denominator is on the left. Write its oriented endpoints and the direction as

$$\frac p\ell<\theta<\frac rs,\qquad \ell<s,\qquad r\ell-ps=1,\qquad m=\lfloor n/\ell\rfloor.$$

The reflected direction may lie in the lower half-plane; $\theta$ is still measured in turns. Put

$$A=2\pi(\ell\theta-p),\qquad B=\frac{2\pi(r-s\theta)}m.$$

Both angles are positive. As $\theta$ moves across the interval, $A+B$ is affine, with endpoint values $2\pi/(m\ell)$ and $2\pi/s$. For $n\geq4$, both $m\ell$ and $s$ are at least three, so $A+B<\pi$.

Define the candidate radius $\widehat R_n(\theta)$ as the unique $\rho\in(0,1)$ satisfying

$$\boxed{\rho^{s/m}\sin A+\rho^\ell\sin B=\sin(A+B).}\tag{R}$$

The left side is strictly increasing in $\rho$, is zero at zero, and at one exceeds the right side. The latter assertion follows from

$$\sin A+\sin B-\sin(A+B) =4\sin(A/2)\sin(B/2)\sin((A+B)/2)>0.$$

Set $\widehat R_n=1$ at the Farey landmarks, and undo any conjugation when returning to the original direction. The hat is deliberate: we have defined a candidate, not assumed it is the boundary.

Every scalar candidate has a stochastic realization

For the root $\rho$ of (R), set $\lambda=\rho e^{2\pi i\theta}$ and

$$t=\frac{\rho^\ell\sin B}{\sin(A+B)},\qquad 1-t=\frac{\rho^{s/m}\sin A}{\sin(A+B)}.$$

Equation (R) makes these positive numbers sum to one. Comparing real and imaginary parts gives

$$\lambda^\ell-t =\frac{\rho^\ell\sin A}{\sin(A+B)}e^{i(A+B)} =(1-t)\rho^{\ell-s/m}e^{i(A+B)}.$$

Write $e=s-\ell m$. The real angle identity

$$2\pi e\theta+m(A+B)=2\pi(r-mp)$$

then proves

$$\lambda^e(\lambda^\ell-t)^m=(1-t)^m.$$

The positive-power polynomial after cancelling the common zero factor has degree

$$D=\max(s,\ell m)\leq n.$$

Also $s>n-\ell\geq(m-1)\ell$. Thus the explicit chain matrix proved in Stop 6 applies and realizes this nonzero root at order $D$; padding gives order $n$. This establishes attainability of the scalar candidate independently of any boundary conclusion.

Why a new radial extremum must have this radius

Let $N\geq4$ be the least order realizing a nonreal strict-contraction radial extremum. Stops 5 and 7 give, in the orientation just chosen,

$$ (\lambda^\ell-t)x_{j-1}=(1-t)x_j\quad(1\leq j\leq m), \qquad \lambda^e x_m=x_0,\qquad e=s-\ell m.$$

The consecutive base directions differ by angles in $(0,\pi)$. Since the weights agree, all these angles equal

$$u=\operatorname{Arg}(\lambda^\ell-t)\in(0,\pi).$$

Crucially, the angular lifts give an equality of real numbers, not only an equality modulo a full turn:

$$\boxed{2\pi e\theta+mu=2\pi(r-mp).}\tag{W}$$

Here is the winding check in both patterns. In the forward pattern, $s=N$, $r=k$, and $k\ell-pN=1$. With $e=N-\ell m$, the closing path has index advance

$$m+ek=(k-mp)N.$$

Its lifted angle therefore gives (W). In the gcd pattern before reflection, put $g=\gcd(N,k)=m$, $\ell=N/g$, and let $f/b$ be the predecessor of $k/N$. Write $\sigma=N-b$. In radians, Stop 7's lifted gap sum is

$$\Gamma=2\pi\left(\frac{g+k\sigma}{N}-\sigma\theta_0\right),$$

where $\theta_0$ is the original direction. After conjugation and reversing the bases, $\theta=1-\theta_0$, $s=b$, $r=b-f$, $p=\ell-k/g$, and $e=-\sigma$. Since $kb-Nf=g$, we obtain

$$2\pi e\theta+\Gamma =2\pi\left(\frac{g+k\sigma}{N}-\sigma\right) =2\pi(k-f-\sigma)=2\pi(r-mp).$$

The reversed base gaps sum to $mu=\Gamma$, proving (W) here too. The single-contact case is included in the forward pattern by the proof in Stop 7.

Solving (W) for $u$ gives $u=A+B$. Taking moduli in the product relation gives

$$|\lambda^\ell-t|=(1-t)\rho^{\ell-s/m}.$$

The imaginary part of the common-weight relation yields $1-t=\rho^{s/m}\sin A/\sin(A+B)$; its real part then yields $t=\rho^\ell\sin B/\sin(A+B)$. Adding proves (R). Hence every such new extremum has radius $\widehat R_N$.

This is the branch-selection statement needed later. A polynomial equation and an angular sector alone would lose the integer winding in (W).

Lemma 9.4Keeping the endpoints and adding a factor moves the candidate outward

Fix the endpoints and direction, and compare factor counts $m$ and $m+1$. Put $C=2\pi(r-s\theta)>0$, so the two values of $B$ are $C/m$ and $C/(m+1)$. Assume $A+C/m<\pi$, as in every application below.

Let $\rho$ be the old scalar root. Define

$$L=-s\log\rho>0,\qquad h(x)=\frac{e^{-Lx}-\cos(Cx)}{\sin(Cx)}.$$

For $0<Cx<\pi$,

$$h'(x)=\frac{C\left[1-e^{-Lx}\left(\cos(Cx)+\frac LC\sin(Cx)\right)\right]}{\sin^2(Cx)}>0.$$

Indeed, $\cos(Cx)+(L/C)\sin(Cx)\leq1+Lx<e^{Lx}$. Dividing the old scalar equation by $\sin A\sin(C/m)$ gives

$$h(1/m)=\frac{\cos A-\rho^\ell}{\sin A}.$$

At the new count, its left side minus right side, evaluated at the old $\rho$, is

$$\sin A\sin\frac C{m+1}\left[h\!\left(\frac1{m+1}\right)-h\!\left(\frac1m\right)\right]<0.$$

The scalar left side is strictly increasing in the radius, so its new root is strictly larger. Thus increasing the factor count really moves the entire open candidate arc outward.

Lemma 9.5Inserting a mediant moves both new candidates outward

Let $a/b<c/d$ be neighbors at order $n-1$ with $b+d=n$, so their mediant $\xi=(a+c)/n$ appears at order $n$. Reflect if necessary to arrange $b<d$. For the old interval put

$$m=\lfloor(n-1)/b\rfloor,\qquad A=2\pi(b\theta-a),\qquad B=\frac{2\pi(c-d\theta)}m.$$

Let $\rho$ solve its scalar equation. Since $mB-A=2\pi((a+c)-n\theta)$, the left new interval has $A<mB$ and the right one has $A>mB$.

New interval Oriented scalar angles Factor count used first
$a/b<\theta<\xi$ $A$, $B-A/m$ $m$
$\xi<\theta<c/d$, reflected $mB$, $A-mB$ $1$

Both angle pairs are positive with sum below $\pi$. Evaluate their right side minus left side at the old $\rho$:

$$\begin{aligned} D_L&=\sin(A+B-A/m)-\rho^{n/m}\sin A-\rho^b\sin(B-A/m),\\ D_R&=\sin A-\rho^n\sin(mB)-\rho^d\sin(A-mB). \end{aligned}$$

We can prove both signs by finite geometric sums. Set $\eta=A/m$, $u=\rho^{b/m}$, and $v=\rho^{d/m}$. Using the old equation

$$v\sin A+u^m\sin B=\sin(A+B),$$

first eliminate $v$ from $D_L$. The result factors as

$$\begin{aligned} D_L&=\sin(B+(m-1)\eta)-u\sin(B+m\eta)\\ &\quad-u^m\sin(B-\eta)+u^{m+1}\sin B\\ &=\operatorname{Im}\!\left[e^{iB}(e^{-i\eta}-u)(e^{im\eta}-u^m)\right]\\ &=|e^{i\eta}-u|^2\sum_{j=0}^{m-1}u^j\sin\bigl(B+(m-1-j)\eta\bigr)>0. \end{aligned}$$

Every sine has argument between $B$ and $B+(m-1)\eta<A+B<\pi$, so every summand is positive.

For $D_R$, eliminate $u^m$ instead and use

$$\sin(A+B)\sin(mB)+\sin B\sin(A-mB) =\sin A\sin((m+1)B).$$

This gives

$$\begin{aligned} \frac{\sin B}{\sin A}D_R &=\sin B-v^m\sin((m+1)B)+v^{m+1}\sin(mB)\\ &=\operatorname{Im}\!\left[e^{iB}(1-ve^{-iB})(1-v^m e^{imB})\right]\\ &=|1-ve^{iB}|^2\sum_{j=0}^{m-1}v^j\sin((j+1)B)>0. \end{aligned}$$

On the right new interval, $0<(j+1)B\leq mB<A<\pi$. Again every summand is positive. Both squared factors are nonzero because $0<u,v<1$.

Thus both new scalar roots exceed the old radius. The right factor count is already correct, since $n=b+d<2d$. On the left, $\lfloor n/b\rfloor$ differs from $m$ only if $b$ divides $n$, hence divides $d$. Coprimality makes this possible only for $b=1$; in that case the factor count increases once, and the preceding comparison increases the radius further. At the inserted landmark itself, the new radius is one and the old radius is less than one.

These comparisons cover every change of order

An old Farey interval either remains intact or splits at its mediant. To check this precisely, if a new fraction $h/n$ lies between $a/b<c/d$, then

$$n=(cn-dh)b+(bh-an)d.$$

Both coefficients are positive integers. Since $b+d\geq n$, equality forces $b+d=n$ and both coefficients to equal one. Thus $h=a+c$; there is exactly one inserted landmark.

If the interval stays intact, its scalar formula changes only when its smaller denominator $\ell$ divides $n$, because only then does $\lfloor n/\ell\rfloor$ increase. We have therefore proved

$$\boxed{\widehat R_{n-1}(\theta)\leq\widehat R_n(\theta),}$$

with strict inequality inside every split interval and inside every unchanged interval whose factor count increases. Otherwise the two formulas, and their roots, agree. Old landmarks retain radius one.

This is exactly the divisibility rule from section 2. For unchanged endpoints, a larger denominator $s<n$ cannot divide $n$: if it did, $n\geq2s$, contradicting $n<\ell+s<2s$. For a split interval, a new endpoint has denominator $n$. Hence an open order-$n$ arc is new exactly when an endpoint denominator divides $n$.

Lemma 9.6Construct a strict witness for every affected old point Karpelevich §5(c)

Assume the smaller-order boundary is known and fix any nonreal old outer point $\mu$ in one of the sectors where the comparison is strict. Let $\theta$ be its direction. The previous calculation gives a realizable candidate on the same ray,

$$\lambda=\widehat R_n(\theta)e^{2\pi i\theta},\qquad \mu=c\lambda,\qquad 0<c<1.$$

Use the explicit chain matrix for $\lambda$, take a nonzero eigenvector, and let $Q$ be the convex hull of its coordinates. It has at most $D\leq n$ vertices and $\lambda Q\subseteq Q$. Since $\lambda$ is nonreal, Stop 1 puts zero in the interior of this two-dimensional polygon.

Choose $r_Q>0$ with the closed disk $\overline B(0,r_Q)\subseteq Q$. Convexity gives

$$cQ+\overline B(0,(1-c)r_Q)\subseteq Q.$$

Consequently, for every vertex $v$ of $Q$,

$$\boxed{\mu v=c\lambda v\in cQ,\qquad \operatorname{dist}(\mu v,\partial Q)\geq(1-c)r_Q>0.}$$

This is the required general interior-image construction, with a uniform positive margin at all image vertices. Section 3 now supplies a neighborhood of $\mu$ inside $\mathcal K_n$. In particular, every affected old boundary point becomes interior.

The construction uses only an explicitly realized candidate and the strict scalar comparison; it does not assume that candidate is already the boundary. It supplies the geometric assertion compressed on p. 382 of Karpelevich's paper through a fully written comparison argument.

We can now prove $R_n=\widehat R_n$, so the candidates just compared are the actual radial frontier. This also excludes every competing polynomial root from lying farther out.

Start the induction without leaving an order-four gap

Stop 8 proved

$$\mathcal K_2=[-1,1],\qquad \mathcal K_3=\operatorname{conv}\{1,\omega,\omega^2\}\cup[-1,1], \quad\omega=e^{2\pi i/3}.$$

On the nonreal rays, the order-three frontier also satisfies the scalar formula. In $(0,1/3)$ it is the segment from $1$ to $\omega$, obtained from $\lambda=t+(1-t)\omega$. Here $(\ell,s,m)=(1,3,3)$. In $(1/3,1/2)$ it is the vertical edge $\operatorname{Re}\lambda=-1/2$. Writing $\lambda=-1/2+iy$ gives

$$\lambda^3-t\lambda=1-t,\qquad t=3/4-y^2,\qquad 0<y<\sqrt3/2.$$

After reflection, $(\ell,s,m)=(2,3,1)$ and the reflected direction is $\theta^{\prime}=1-\theta$. Here $A+B=2\pi(1-\theta^{\prime})=2\pi\theta$, while $\overline\lambda^{\,2}-t=\lambda$. Its factor therefore has argument exactly $A+B$, establishing the real winding used in (R). Thus this is precisely its scalar root. In these open intervals $A+B<\pi$, so both comparison lemmas apply even though the order-three real-axis spike has different behavior.

From $F_3$ to $F_4$, the interval $(0,1/3)$ splits at $1/4$, while the interval $(1/3,1/2)$ keeps its endpoints and its factor count rises from one to two. Hence $\widehat R_4$ dominates the entire nonreal order-three frontier. The real rays have radius one at both orders.

The candidate $\widehat R_4$ is realizable. An order-four radial maximum either is old, in which case the preceding comparison bounds it, or is new, in which case the contact relations and winding calculation force its radius to be $\widehat R_4$. Therefore $R_4=\widehat R_4$.

The inductive step has both inclusions

Assume $R_{n-1}=\widehat R_{n-1}$ for $n\geq5$, and fix a nonreal direction. Attainability already gives

$$R_n\geq\widehat R_n.$$

Let $\lambda=R_n e^{2\pi i\theta}$ be the radial maximum, which exists by compactness. If $\lambda\in\mathcal K_{n-1}$, nesting and the induction hypothesis give

$$R_n\leq R_{n-1}=\widehat R_{n-1}\leq\widehat R_n.$$

If $\lambda\notin\mathcal K_{n-1}$, it is a new radial extremum at order $n$. For a unit-circle point the radius is one. Otherwise the common-weight relations and (W) prove that its radius is the unique scalar root $\widehat R_n$ in its Farey sector. Thus $R_n\leq\widehat R_n$ here too.

At a Farey landmark, the corresponding root of unity is realized by a permutation matrix, and every stochastic eigenvalue has modulus at most one. The real endpoints are $1$ and $-1$. This completes the induction:

$$\boxed{R_n(\theta)=\widehat R_n(\theta)\quad(n\geq4).}$$

On unchanged intervals with unchanged factor count, equality of scalar formulas proves inheritance. Where the comparison is strict, the general polygon construction proves that the old frontier is interior, and the new frontier is outside $\mathcal K_{n-1}$. These are precisely the two cases announced in section 2.

How the ancestry explorer’s comparison order is computed

With the induction established, the ancestry explorer’s smaller comparison orders can be recovered from the same Farey data. Recall the chosen-orientation sectors:

$$S_{n,k}=\left(\frac fb,\frac kn\right),$$

where the predecessor is specified by

$$\boxed{kb-nf=g,\qquad g=\gcd(k,n),\qquad0<n-b<n/g.}$$

Thus $f/b$ is the Farey predecessor of the reduced fraction $k/n$. The opposite contact orientation is handled by conjugation.

Karpelevich first identifies the old boundary visible in each such sector with a boundary at a smaller comparison order $n_*<n$:

$$\Gamma_{n-1}\cap S_{n,k}=\Gamma_{n_*}\cap S_{n,k}.$$

Here intersections with an interval mean intersections with its open angular sector. In a compact version of the original bookkeeping,

$$n_*= \begin{cases} \ell\left\lfloor(n-1)/\ell\right\rfloor,&g=1,\\ b,&g>1, \end{cases}$$

where $\ell$ is the smaller denominator of the two Farey neighbors bracketing $k/n$ when it is reduced, and $b$ is the predecessor denominator when $g>1$.

For example, at $n=6,k=2$, the sector is $(1/4,1/3)$ and $n_*=4$: it is precisely the old order-four arc that is visible at order five. At $n=8,k=3$, the bracketing denominators are three and five, giving $n_*=3\lfloor7/3\rfloor=6$.

If $g=1$, the two neighbors bracketing $k/n$ have denominators $b_-,b_+$ with $b_-+b_+=n$. They were neighbors in $F_{n-1}$. Put $\ell=\min(b_-,b_+)$ and $s=\max(b_-,b_+)$. Then $s\leq n_*\leq n-1$, the pair is still neighboring in $F_{n_*}$, and division by either denominator gives the same quotient at $n_*$ as at $n-1$. The special end sectors give $n_*=n-1$.

If $g>1$, write $k/n=c/d$ in lowest terms, so $n=gd$. The predecessor denominator satisfies $n-d<b<n$. In particular $b>n/2$ and $\lfloor b/d\rfloor=\lfloor(n-1)/d\rfloor=g-1$. The same Farey interval and quotient data already occur at order $b$.

The two endpoint fractions remain adjacent at the stated comparison order, and the smaller-denominator factor count agrees. The scalar formulas are therefore identical. The equality $R_j=\widehat R_j$, now proved for all smaller orders, gives the displayed old-boundary comparison. These are the divisions checked in §5(c), equations (30)–(34), of the original paper.

Why this is the topological boundary

Inside each open Farey sector, the scalar equation has positive derivative with respect to $\rho$. Its implicit root is therefore continuous and smooth. At the left endpoint, $A\to0$ and $B\to2\pi/(m\ell)\in(0,\pi)$, so (R) forces every limiting radius to satisfy $\rho^\ell=1$. At the right endpoint, $B\to0$ and $A\to2\pi/s\in(0,\pi)$, so the limit satisfies $\rho^{s/m}=1$. Both limits are one.

Thus $R_n$ is a continuous positive function on the circle for $n\geq4$. Star-shapedness from Stop 1 gives

$$\mathcal K_n=\{re^{2\pi i\theta}:0\leq r\leq R_n(\theta)\}.$$

Every point with $0<r<R_n(\theta)$ is interior: continuity leaves a positive radial gap on nearby directions. The origin is interior too, because the positive continuous function $R_n$ has a positive minimum. Every point with $r=R_n(\theta)$ is on the boundary, since every outward radial move leaves the region. Therefore

$$\boxed{\partial\mathcal K_n =\{R_n(\theta)e^{2\pi i\theta}:0\leq\theta<1\},\qquad n\geq4.}$$

This identifies the radial frontier with the topological boundary under the correct order hypothesis. The real-axis spike at order three is why that hypothesis matters.

The parameter traces exactly one simple arc

The scalar root gives a smooth curve $\lambda(\theta)=\rho(\theta)e^{2\pi i\theta}$. Its argument is the chosen direction, so the curve is simple and

$$\lambda'(\theta)=e^{2\pi i\theta}\bigl(\rho'(\theta)+2\pi i\rho(\theta)\bigr)\ne0.$$

The weight $t(\theta)=\rho^\ell\sin B/\sin(A+B)$ tends to one at the left oriented endpoint and zero at the right. It is strictly decreasing. To prove this last assertion, set

$$G(\lambda,t)=\lambda^e(\lambda^\ell-t)^m-(1-t)^m,\qquad e=s-\ell m.$$

At our nonzero roots, even when $e<0$, this function is differentiable and

$$G_\lambda=\lambda^{e-1}(\lambda^\ell-t)^{m-1} \bigl(s\lambda^\ell-e t\bigr)\ne0.$$

The last factor has imaginary part $s\rho^\ell\sin A>0$; the other factors are nonzero as well. Differentiating $G(\lambda(\theta),t(\theta))=0$ shows that $t'(\theta)$ cannot vanish, since $G_\lambda\lambda'\ne0$. Its continuous derivative consequently has constant sign; the endpoint limits force that sign to be negative.

Thus $t$ traverses $(0,1)$ exactly once along the oriented arc. Undoing conjugation reverses the traversal where appropriate. We have proved the existence, selection, and monotone parameterization of the boundary branch. Other roots of the polynomial remain realizable by Stop 6, but the completed upper bound prevents them from lying farther out on any ray.

The equations retain the correct realizing degree

For every order-$n$ Farey interval, let $\ell<s$ be the oriented endpoint denominators and $m=\lfloor n/\ell\rfloor$. Its boundary branch is selected by (R) and (W) from

$$\lambda^s(\lambda^\ell-t)^m=(1-t)^m\lambda^{\ell m}.$$

This is the unified equation. Cancelling its common zero factor gives the following reduced polynomials:

Exponent comparison Reduced relation Realizing degree
$s\geq\ell m$ $\lambda^{s-\ell m}(\lambda^\ell-t)^m=(1-t)^m$ $D=s$
$s<\ell m$ $(\lambda^\ell-t)^m=(1-t)^m\lambda^{\ell m-s}$ $D=\ell m$

Always $D=\max(s,\ell m)\leq n$. For a new open order-$n$ arc, $D=n$: either a new endpoint has denominator $s=n$, or a retained smaller denominator divides $n$, so $\ell m=n$. An inherited arc can have $D<n$.

Figure 5. Angular coverage rules out new extrema in the inherited case. In a new sector, the smaller-order comparison and strict-interior construction rule out old outer points. These complementary exclusions complete the selection of the boundary pieces.

For the running example, the equations are

$$\begin{array}{ll} n=4,5:&\lambda^4=t\lambda+(1-t),\\ n=6:&(\lambda^3-t)^2=(1-t)^2\lambda^2. \end{array}$$

In the open sector $(1/4,1/3)$, the second relation selects $\lambda^3+(1-t)\lambda-t=0$: the imaginary part of $\lambda^3$ is negative, so the negative square-root sign is forced. Selecting that cubic factor retains the six-state realization of the full characteristic relation.

An exact way to draw the two worked arcs

The order-four polynomial has a factor $\lambda-1$. Factoring its remaining cubic at its real root $-u$ gives, for $0\leq u\leq1$,

$$\lambda_4(u)=\frac{u-1+i\sqrt{3-2u+3u^2}}2, \qquad t_4(u)=(1-u)(1+u^2).$$

For the selected order-six cubic, let $u$ be its positive real root. Then

$$t_6(u)=\frac{u(1+u^2)}{1+u},\qquad \lambda_6(u)=-\frac u2+ \frac i2\sqrt{\frac{4+3u^2-u^3}{1+u}}.$$

Both formulas are exact substitutions into the displayed equations. Their drawing coordinates are evaluated numerically. The inductive argument, rather than the drawing, establishes their boundary role.

Taking the sectors in circular order, adjoining their unit-circle endpoints, and reflecting across the real axis now gives Theorem B, including a proof that these curves are the entire boundary.

After the proof: modern formulations and computations

The necessity, attainability, order comparison, and topological-boundary claims have now been proved from the preceding stops and the calculations above. Modern work gives useful complementary formulations and realizations.

  • Ito (1997) gives the simpler equivalent polynomial formulation. The unified equation above is reduced only after its common zero factor is cancelled.
  • Johnson–Paparella (2017) give explicit parameterized stochastic realizations. The constructive graph and determinant argument used here are written out in Stop 6.
  • Kirkland–Laffey–Šmigoc (2020) give a useful polar characterization of the established region.
  • Munger–Nickerson–Paparella (2024) study continuous arc selection and extremality, clarifying why those questions must be distinguished from stochastic realization alone.

The scalar-comparison route is also developed in Verbeken–Ginis (2026), §§5–6. Here the factor-count comparison is proved by a one-variable derivative and the mediant comparison by explicit positive finite sums. This is an expanded algebraic proof of the final exclusion, followed by an explicit strict polygon witness; it is not a transcription of the brief geometric construction in the 1951 paper.

What the series has established

The original proof gives two descriptions of the same region. Theorem A covers it with cyclic polygons. Theorem B identifies its outside through ordered unit-circle landmarks and the equations of the intervening arcs.

The chip game prepared a polygon whose chains could be read arithmetically. Variations forced the common weights. Elimination produced equations, and angles placed their roots. The last induction decides which equations remain visible at each order.

The final selection rests on both sides of the argument: the inherited sectors admit no new extremum, while the new sectors admit no surviving old outer point. That completes the route from stochastic matrix rows to the Karpelevich boundary.
